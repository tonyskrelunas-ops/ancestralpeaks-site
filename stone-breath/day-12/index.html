    Today's challenge · Tool 12 of 22

    The Honorable Harvest

    Before you take anything today, ask: is it given, am I leaving enough, what am I giving back?

    The tool, as the book gives it

    Origin: Navajo – Ada’s dawn ceremony / Lithuanian – Ona’s forest walks.

    Before you take anything – from the land, from a person, from an opportunity – ask three questions:

    Am I taking only what is given? – Not what I want. Not what I can take. What is freely offered.

    Am I leaving enough for the next season? – Enough for the plants to regrow. Enough for the child to have a turn. Enough for the next person who comes.

    Am I offering something in return? – A prayer. A gift. A commitment to reciprocity.

    If all three answers are yes, proceed. If any is no, pause. Wait. Ask again.

    For the reader. Tonas taught Lukas the stone breath. He didn’t promise to stay. He just showed up. He listened. He gave him the stone. That was the Honorable Harvest.

    Study · Day 12

    Giving and taking: what the research found, and what it did not

    The grandmothers taught it as manners, toward the land and toward each other. Researchers came at it from the other side: what happens to people when giving and taking fall out of balance. Here are the papers, so you can read them yourself.

    Effort without return wears on the heart

    A model of stress at work puts reciprocity at its center: high cost with low gain is the hard condition. In a study that followed blue-collar men over time, high effort together with low reward predicted new heart and blood-vessel events, apart from the other risks measured.

    What it did not show: this is working life, and men in industrial jobs. It is about being on the short end of an exchange for years, not about taking with care.

    Siegrist, J. (1996). Adverse health effects of high-effort/low-reward conditions. Journal of Occupational Health Psychology, 1(1), 27–41. doi.org/10.1037//1076-8998.1.1.27

    Those who gave lived longer

    Older married adults were followed for five years. Those who reported giving practical help to friends, relatives and neighbors, and emotional support to their spouse, were less likely to die in that time. Receiving support made no difference once giving was counted.

    What it did not show: the people were watched, not assigned. Someone well enough to give may be healthier to begin with, though the researchers accounted for health, mood and personality.

    Brown, S. L., Nesse, R. M., Vinokur, A. D., & Smith, D. M. (2003). Providing social support may be more beneficial than receiving it: Results from a prospective study of mortality. Psychological Science, 14(4), 320–327. doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.14461

    It is not a simple ledger

    A study of 178 men and 575 women compared the support each gave and received in their closest relationships with their recorded sick days over nine years. Women who gave more than they received had fewer sick days. Men who received more than they gave had fewer. An even balance was not what predicted health.

    What it did not show: that balance itself protects anyone. The authors found no support for that idea, and the result differed for women and men.

    Väänänen, A., Buunk, B. P., Kivimäki, M., Pentti, J., & Vahtera, J. (2005). When it is better to give than to receive: Long-term health effects of perceived reciprocity in support exchange. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 89(2), 176–193. doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.89.2.176

    A small gift, tested by chance

    In a national survey and in a study of people who received a windfall, spending more on others went with more happiness. Then the researchers tested it: people assigned by chance to spend money on someone else were happier afterward than those assigned to spend it on themselves.

    What it did not show: it measured happiness, not health, and money, not time or care. It says nothing about the land.

    Dunn, E. W., Aknin, L. B., & Norton, M. I. (2008). Spending money on others promotes happiness. Science, 319(5870), 1687–1688. doi.org/10.1126/science.1150952

    Where the Honorable Harvest sits in this

    The Honorable Harvest is three questions asked before taking: is it given, am I leaving enough, what am I giving back. The research above is about people giving to and taking from people. It suggests that giving is good for the giver, and that years on the short end of an exchange are hard on the body.

    No study here tested the three questions, or taking from the land with care. One study found that an even balance was not what predicted health. The Honorable Harvest does not ask for an even ledger either. It asks for a relationship, and that part is the grandmothers’ teaching, not a laboratory’s.

    This is a practice, not medical care.

    The passage to keep

    “Everything else in your life is grading you on what you did. These ones grade you on what you are. They are never wrong, they are never cruel about it, and they will tell you the same thing at nine and at fifty.”

    “I honor him here and he honors me there. That is not generosity, it is the only arithmetic that works. Honor begets honor.”

    Tonas to Ugnė, and Austėja on the porch steps. Stone Breath, Chapter 12.

    Three questions to think on or write about

    1. Name one thing you took this week without asking whether it was given: someone’s time, their attention, their patience. What would asking have changed?
    2. “Sit down where something alive is. Be still long enough to be noticed.” Where is that place for you, and what would it say about you today?
    3. Austėja keeps Kenji’s half alive in her kitchen, every week, on purpose. Whose half are you keeping alive for someone, and whose are you leaving out?
