    Today's challenge · Tool 6 of 22

    The Talka Protocol

    Name one job that is too big for one person. Ask two people to help, and feed them.

    The tool, as the book gives it

    Origin: Lithuanian traditional community labor + Navajo showing-up.

    Identify a need too big for one person. Invite everyone – call, knock, text. Assign roles. No one stands idle. Feed everyone – the meal is as important as the work. Sing at the end. That is how the tradition continues.

    The talka is not about efficiency. It is about belonging.

    The talka is not a revival. It is a remembering. Your ancestors did this. You have done this. You will do this again. The building may fall. The talka never does.

    Before you build, you breathe. At the mill, before anyone picked up a hammer, Austėja gathered all forty-seven in a circle: “Breathe in, light and easy. Feel the forest. Breathe out, slow. Let go of what you’re carrying.”

    From “The Complete Toolkit” and Chapter 7 of Stone Breath.

    Study · Day 7

    What the research found, and what it did not

    Rytis says it at the foundation: “You think you know community. Watch.” Here is what has been counted since about people who have others around them, and about what moving in time together does, with the papers so you can read them yourself.

    People with stronger ties lived longer

    A review from Brigham Young University gathered 148 studies that had followed 308,849 people. Those with stronger social relationships had a 50% greater likelihood of survival, and that held across age, sex, starting health, cause of death and length of follow-up. In the paper’s own words, the size of the effect “is comparable with quitting smoking.”

    What it did not show: cause. These studies followed people over time. No one can be assigned at random to have friends, and the authors say so themselves.

    Holt-Lunstad, J., Smith, T. B., & Layton, J. B. (2010). Social relationships and mortality risk: A meta-analytic review. PLoS Medicine, 7(7), e1000316. doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316

    Lonely, cut off, or living alone

    Five years later the same group looked at the lack itself. Across studies that held other risks steady, social isolation went with a 29% greater likelihood of early death, loneliness with 26%, and living alone with 32%. It made no difference whether a person was alone by the count or only felt alone.

    What it did not show: again, a link over time and not proof of cause; a person’s health at the start changed the result. The link was stronger in groups whose average age was under 65.

    Holt-Lunstad, J., Smith, T. B., Baker, M., Harris, T., & Stephenson, D. (2015). Loneliness and social isolation as risk factors for mortality: A meta-analytic review. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 10(2), 227–237. doi.org/10.1177/1745691614568352

    Moving in time, people gave more

    In three experiments, people who first acted in synchrony with others then cooperated more in group exercises with money at stake, even when it cost them personally. They did not have to feel good for it to work.

    What it did not show: these were short exercises in a laboratory, not three days at a mill. What they show is a direction, not a size.

    Wiltermuth, S. S., & Heath, C. (2009). Synchrony and cooperation. Psychological Science, 20(1), 1–5. doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02253.x

    Dancing together, strangers felt closer

    Strangers danced either in full synchrony, partly, or out of time. Those in synchrony came away with a higher pain threshold and reported feeling more bonded to each other.

    What it did not show: the pain threshold stands in for endorphins, which were not measured directly. And the people who danced in time did not act more generously in a money game afterwards.

    Tarr, B., Launay, J., & Dunbar, R. I. M. (2016). Silent disco: Dancing in synchrony leads to elevated pain thresholds and social closeness. Evolution and Human Behavior, 37(5), 343–349. doi.org/10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2016.02.004

    Where the talka sits in this

    The toolkit says: “Loneliness has the same mortality risk as 15 cigarettes a day (Brigham Young University).” The Brigham Young work is the first two reviews above. The cigarette line is the short form this finding is usually passed along in; the 2010 paper’s own sentence, quoted above, is the comparison with quitting smoking.

    No study here tested a talka. The call, the roles, the meal and the song at the end come from the villages that kept them. What the research adds is a count of what it costs to go without.

    This is a practice, not medical care. If you are alone today and it is heavy, one call counts as the whole challenge.

    Three questions to think on or write about

    1. Tonas learns he had been “a piece of good news in a place he had never seen, read out in a road, before he could walk.” Whose good news have you been without knowing it? Who is yours?
    2. “You didn’t tell me. You just believed.” “That’s the same thing.” What are you carrying alone because you have not believed anyone would come?
    3. Rytis: “You have to give something back. That’s the rule.” When you have been helped, what do you give back: a song, a meal, a dance you are willing to look foolish doing?
