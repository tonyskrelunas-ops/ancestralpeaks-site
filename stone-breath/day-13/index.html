    Today's challenge · Tool 13 of 22

    Ichigo Ichie

    Before one meeting today, take one breath and say to yourself: this meeting will never come again.

    The tool, as the book gives it

    Origin: Japanese tea ceremony tradition.

    Before any encounter – a meeting, a meal, a conversation, a competition – pause for one breath.

    Say it to yourself: “This meeting will never come again.” Then walk in.

    Be fully present. Not because you have to get it right. Because it will never happen again. Not because the other person will leave – because you won’t be the same person tomorrow. Time moves. You move. Everything moves.

    For the reader. Tonas had given a hundred speeches. But this one – in Kyoto, with Austėja and Ugnė in the audience – would never come again. Not because he would never return to Japan. Because he would never be this person again. That’s what ichigo ichie means. Not “treasure every moment.” It means: this moment is all you have. Don’t waste it wishing for another.

    Study · Day 13

    The mind that is somewhere else: what the research found, and what it did not

    The tea teachers had the saying for centuries. Here is what has been measured since about a mind that is somewhere else, in plain words, with the papers so you can read them yourself.

    Elsewhere, almost half the time

    Researchers used a phone to ask people, at moments through their day, what they were doing, what they were thinking about and how they felt. People were thinking about something other than what was happening almost as often as they were thinking about what was, and doing so typically left them less happy.

    What it did not show: the answers are people’s own reports, caught in passing. The study tested no practice; it only found the pattern.

    Killingsworth, M. A., & Gilbert, D. T. (2010). A wandering mind is an unhappy mind. Science, 330(6006), 932. doi.org/10.1126/science.1192439

    The brain has a resting pattern

    Brain scans of adults resting quietly showed a set of areas that is steadily active at rest and quiets down whenever a person turns to a task that has a goal. The authors named it a default mode of brain function.

    What it did not show: that this pattern is a fault. The paper described a baseline; it did not say the baseline should be switched off.

    Raichle, M. E., MacLeod, A. M., Snyder, A. Z., Powers, W. J., Gusnard, D. A., & Shulman, G. L. (2001). A default mode of brain function. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 98(2), 676–682. doi.org/10.1073/pnas.98.2.676

    Long practice, a quieter default

    Experienced meditators and matched people new to meditation were scanned during several kinds of meditation. In the experienced group the main parts of the default mode network were less active, and regions tied to self-monitoring and control were more strongly linked, which the authors read as fitting with less mind-wandering.

    What it did not show: these were people with long experience, compared once with beginners. It was not one breath, and it cannot say the practice made the difference.

    Brewer, J. A., Worhunsky, P. D., Gray, J. R., Tang, Y.-Y., Weber, J., & Kober, H. (2011). Meditation experience is associated with differences in default mode network activity and connectivity. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 108(50), 20254–20259. doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1112029108

    Two weeks of training

    In a randomized, controlled study, a two-week mindfulness course cut down mind-wandering during a test and improved both reading-comprehension scores and working memory. The gain came through less mind-wandering in the people who had been most prone to it.

    What it did not show: it was a course of two weeks, measured on a test, not a single breath at a door.

    Mrazek, M. D., Franklin, M. S., Phillips, D. T., Baird, B., & Schooler, J. W. (2013). Mindfulness training improves working memory capacity and GRE performance while reducing mind wandering. Psychological Science, 24(5), 776–781. doi.org/10.1177/0956797612459659

    Where ichigo ichie sits in this

    Ichigo ichie is one breath and one sentence at the door. The research above supports the ground it stands on: the mind is somewhere else much of the time, it tends to be less happy there, and attention can be trained.

    The book says the one-breath pause interrupts the default mode network. That is a fair picture of where the research points; it has not been measured. The studies looked at people with long practice or at a two-week course, never at a single breath at a threshold. The saying, and the bowl mended with gold, belong to the tea teachers.

    This is a practice, not medical care.

    “In Japan, you have a word – ichigo ichie. One lifetime, one meeting. This meeting – right now, you and me, in this hall, with the cherry blossoms falling outside – will never come again.”
