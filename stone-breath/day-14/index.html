    Today's challenge · Tool 15 of 22

    The Centering

    The stone breath first. Then the hand, the five lines, slowly, once. Do it at a door today, before you walk into anything hard.

    The tool, as the book gives it

    Origin: Ada’s teaching of the hand. She would take your hand and turn it palm-up in her own. One side is your mother’s side, she’d say; one side is your father’s – female and male, the two of them, in a single hand, the way of every living thing. The thumb is where you hold your own safety: when you are afraid, you hold it, and you are holding the part of you that is safe, and loved, and held.

    Breathe the stone breath first – in 4, hold 4, out 6, hold 4. Let your jaw go loose, your shoulders drop, your face soften. Then, slowly, move through the hand:

    Hold your thumb. “I am safe. I am calm. My mind is quiet.”
    Hand over your heart. “The power I was born with is still mine.”
    Touch your forehead. “I am awake.”
    Turn your palm outward. “Help me overcome all my challenges.”
    Fist over your heart, then let it open. “Let me carry out your purpose with positive power.”

    Say each line slowly, and keep the breath soft the whole way through. There is no rush; the calm is the point. Then walk on. Do it again tomorrow.

    When to use it. Before a difficult conversation. When you feel yourself shrinking. After an encounter with someone who drains you. First thing in the morning. At any threshold – literal or metaphorical.

    Study · Day 14

    What the research found, and what it did not

    Ada put the practice in the hand: a touch, then a line said slowly. Here is what has been measured about each half, in plain words, with the papers so you can read them yourself.

    A hand of your own, under stress

    A randomized controlled trial put 159 healthy adults, aged 18 to 35, through a standard laboratory stress test. Before it, some used a self-soothing touch, such as a hand placed on the heart, some received a hug, and some did neither. People in both touch groups had lower cortisol after the stress than the people in the control groups.

    What it did not show: heart rate and how stressed people said they felt did not differ between the groups. It was one session, in one laboratory, with young adults.

    Dreisoerner, A., Junker, N. M., Schlotz, W., Heimrich, J., Bloemeke, S., Ditzen, B., & van Dick, R. (2021). Self-soothing touch and being hugged reduce cortisol responses to stress: A randomized controlled trial on stress, physical touch, and social identity. Comprehensive Psychoneuroendocrinology, 8, 100091. doi.org/10.1016/j.cpnec.2021.100091

    Touch, across many studies

    A pre-registered review and meta-analysis gathered 137 studies in its analysis and 75 more in its review, 12,966 people in all, each comparing touch with no touch. Touch helped body and mind. In adults the clearest effects were less pain, fewer feelings of depression and less anxiety.

    What it did not show: what your own hand does. The studies are of touch received, from a person, a professional, or even an object or a robot, and touch from objects gave smaller benefits for the mind than touch from people.

    Packheiser, J., Hartmann, H., Fredriksen, K., Gazzola, V., Keysers, C., & Michon, F. (2024). A systematic review and multivariate meta-analysis of the physical and mental health benefits of touch interventions. Nature Human Behaviour, 8(6), 1088–1107. doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-01841-8

    Words said to yourself

    A meta-analysis of self-talk in sport pooled 32 studies and 62 effect sizes and found a positive, moderate effect on performance (0.48). It worked better on fine, precise tasks and on new ones, and better when people had been trained in their self-talk than when they had not.

    What it did not show: calm. The measure is performance on sport tasks, and the words are not these five lines. The finding about training does sit well with the book: said daily, not once.

    Hatzigeorgiadis, A., Zourbanos, N., Galanis, E., & Theodorakis, Y. (2011). Self-talk and sports performance: A meta-analysis. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 6(4), 348–356. doi.org/10.1177/1745691611413136

    A caution about positive lines

    Two experiments had people repeat a positive statement about themselves (“I’m a lovable person”). People with high self-esteem felt a little better. People with low self-esteem felt worse than those who did not repeat it. The authors conclude that such statements can backfire for the very people who reach for them most.

    What it did not show: whether the same holds for lines like these, which are joined to a slow breath and a touch, and which include an honest ask for help. Still, the caution belongs on this page: if a line rings false on a hard day, that is worth noticing, not forcing.

    Wood, J. V., Perunovic, W. Q., & Lee, J. W. (2009). Positive self-statements: Power for some, peril for others. Psychological Science, 20(7), 860–866. doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02370.x

    Where the Centering sits in this

    The Centering joins three things these papers study apart: a slow breath (the study on Day 1), a touch of your own hand, and words said to yourself.

    No study here tested the Centering itself, with its five touches and its five lines. The teaching of the hand, the mother’s side and the father’s side, the thumb as the place where you hold your own safety, comes from Ada, not from a laboratory. The book is plain about what the words are: “The words are not a spell. They are a practice that tells your nervous system: I am safe, I am held, I am ready.”

    This is a practice, not medical care.

    The passage to keep

    “Before you walk into anything hard, you stop at the door. Any door. I will show you the way I did it every morning of my life.”

    “That is the whole thing. It is not a spell. It is you, telling your own body. We are here. We are covered. We are ready. Do it every morning. The floor under you changes. Do it again tomorrow.”

    Masan. Stone Breath, Chapter 14.

    Three questions to think on or write about

    1. Tonas hid the heel for weeks, and when Austėja asked why he had said nothing, he answered: “Because I’m an idiot.” What are you limping on that someone close to you has already noticed?
    2. Which door do you walk through most days without stopping? What would change if your hand rested on the frame first?
    3. Austėja says of the plants: “Learn them from a person. Not from a book, and not from me in one afternoon.” What have you learned only because a person stood beside you, year by year? Who is learning that way from you?
