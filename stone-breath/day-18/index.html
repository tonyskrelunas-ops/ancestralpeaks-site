    Today's challenge · Tool 19 of 22, with the Mantra Anchor

    The Mantra Anchor and the Mountain Compass

    Choose your phrase, two to four words, said to yourself as “you”. Say it morning and night. Then write your Mountain List: three things that matter most this season.

    The tools, as the book gives them

    The Mantra Anchor. From athletes who whisper to themselves in second person, and from the old prayers: “calling on yourself as if you were someone worth believing in.”

    Choose your phrase. Two to four words. Present tense. Positive. Examples: “You belong here.” “You got this.” “It’s your time.”

    Practice it daily. In the mirror. On your run. Before sleep. Let it become automatic.

    Deploy it at the moment of pressure. One breath. One whisper. Then move.

    Tool 19 · The Mountain Compass.

    The Mountain List – Write down: three things that matter most in this season of your life, three things that drain you, and three things you would do even if no one watched. This is your mountain. Everything else is foothills.

    The Litmus Test – When an opportunity appears, ask: “Does this feed my mountain or my foothills?” If not, it is a candidate for no.

    The 3-Cube Rule. Wait three days before responding to any non-urgent request. Try three alternatives to immediate engagement: delegate, delay, decline. Ask three trusted voices who know your mountain.

    From the tool page of Chapter 18 and “The Complete Toolkit” (Tool 19) in Stone Breath. The toolkit also gives the weekly pause and the seasonal return.

    Study · Day 18

    What the research found, and what it did not

    Tonas tells Austėja that the brain hears “you” differently than “I”. Here is what has been measured since, in plain words, with the papers so you can read them yourself.

    Talking to yourself as you would to someone else

    Seven studies, 585 people in all. People who used their own name or a pronoun other than “I” while getting ready for a stressful task (making a first impression, giving a speech) were rated as performing better, felt less distress afterward, and saw the coming stress more as a challenge and less as a threat.

    What it did not show: these were short tasks in a laboratory, and “you” was tested together with the person’s own name, not by itself.

    Kross, E., Bruehlman-Senecal, E., Park, J., Burson, A., Dougherty, A., Shablack, H., Bremner, R., Moser, J., & Ayduk, O. (2014). Self-talk as a regulatory mechanism: How you do it matters. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 106(2), 304–324. doi.org/10.1037/a0035173

    “You” against “I”, side by side

    Three experiments compared the two directly. People who gave themselves advice or prepared for a task using “You” did better on word puzzles and meant more strongly to keep at it, and in the third experiment to exercise, than people who used “I”. The difference ran through how they felt about the task.

    What it did not show: word puzzles and intentions are not a competition kitchen or a starting line.

    Dolcos, S., & Albarracín, D. (2014). The inner speech of behavioral regulation: Intentions and task performance strengthen when you talk to yourself as a You. European Journal of Social Psychology, 44(6), 636–642. doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.2048

    Self-talk in sport, added up

    A meta-analysis of 32 studies (62 results) found that self-talk had a moderate, positive effect on performance in sport. It worked better when the athletes had trained it than when they had not, which is the book’s second step: practice it until it is automatic.

    What it did not show: it did not compare “you” with “I”, and the effect was larger for fine, precise tasks and for new tasks than for well-learned ones.

    Hatzigeorgiadis, A., Zourbanos, N., Galanis, E., & Theodorakis, Y. (2011). Self-talk and sports performance: A meta-analysis. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 6(4), 348–356. doi.org/10.1177/1745691611413136

    The fear of missing out, looked at

    A review of the research on the fear of missing out on rewarding experiences: how it is defined and measured, and what it goes with. Across the studies it reviews, more of that fear goes with more time pulled into online social life, with problem use of phones and the internet, and with more anxiety and low mood.

    What it did not show: these are links, not causes, and the review tests no remedy. The Mountain List is the book’s answer, not the review’s.

    Elhai, J. D., Yang, H., & Montag, C. (2021). Fear of missing out (FOMO): Overview, theoretical underpinnings, and literature review on relations with severity of negative affectivity and problematic technology use. Brazilian Journal of Psychiatry, 43(2), 203–209. doi.org/10.1590/1516-4446-2020-0870

    Where the Mantra Anchor and the Mountain Compass sit in this

    The direction of the book’s teaching is supported: stepping a little outside yourself in the words you use, and practicing the words, helps under pressure. No study here tested the Mantra Anchor as the book gives it, and none tested the Mountain Compass. The Mountain List, the three days of waiting and the weekly pause are the book’s teaching, not a laboratory’s.

    The tool page says second-person self-talk “reduces activity in the amygdala.” The closest brain study we found used the person’s own name, not “you”. It found a smaller marker of emotional reactivity within the first second, and less activity in a region tied to thinking about oneself, without extra effort of control. Its summary names no amygdala result. So that sentence is best read as the book’s explanation, not a settled finding.

    Moser, J. S., Dougherty, A., Mattson, W. I., Katz, B., Moran, T. P., Guevarra, D., Shablack, H., Ayduk, O., Jonides, J., Berman, M. G., & Kross, E. (2017). Third-person self-talk facilitates emotion regulation without engaging cognitive control: Converging evidence from ERP and fMRI. Scientific Reports, 7, 4519. doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-04047-3

    This is a practice, not medical care.

    The passage to keep

    “Because your brain hears it differently. When you say ‘you,’ it sounds like someone else believing in you. Someone who’s got your back — a friend saying ‘You belong here,’ not your own inner critic muttering ‘I need to belong here.’ It’s not pressure – it’s permission.”

    “The voice you listen to becomes the voice you believe. So choose the voice.”

    Tonas, at the oak and on the porch. Stone Breath, Chapter 18.

    Three questions to think on or write about

    1. Austėja is not afraid of losing. She is afraid of “being the one who could have and did not.” What is the fear under your own fear?
    2. How do you talk to a friend before a hard thing? How do you talk to yourself? Write both down, word for word.
    3. Look at your Mountain List. What did you say yes to this month that feeds the foothills?
