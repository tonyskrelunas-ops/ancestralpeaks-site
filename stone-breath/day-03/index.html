    Today's challenge · Tool 2 of 22

    The Three Questions

    Tonight, before you sleep, write three answers: what you received today, what you gave, what trouble you caused. That is the whole challenge for today.

    The tool, as the book gives it

    Origin: Japanese Naikan + Lithuanian Rasos (daily reckoning).

    At the end of each day, sit quietly with a journal. Ask:

    What have I received today? – Specific, not abstract. The coffee someone made. The door someone held. The patience someone extended.

    What have I given today? – Not what you intended – what you actually did. Did you listen? Did you show up? Did you hold your tongue instead of snapping?

    What troubles have I caused today? – Was I short with someone? Did I leave a mess? Did I take more than I needed?

    Write each answer. Read them over. Take three slow breaths. Close with: “Tomorrow I will try to give a little more and trouble a little less.”

    “This question is not for shame. It is for clearing. You cannot set down what you will not look at.” Ona, on the third question.

    The Three Questions don’t fix anything – they just let you see. And sometimes, seeing is enough.

    From “The Complete Toolkit” and Ona’s page at the end of Chapter 3, in Stone Breath.

    Study · Day 3

    Counting what you received: what the research found, and what it did not

    Ona kept this accounting through years when little else was hers to keep. Researchers came to the first of her questions much later. Here is what they measured, in plain words, and where the evidence thins out.

    Writing down what you were given

    In two studies, people were randomly assigned to keep lists of things they were grateful for, of hassles, or of something neutral, weekly in one study and daily in the other. A third study did the same with people living with neuromuscular disease. The gratitude groups showed higher well-being on several measures. The steadiest effect was on positive mood.

    What it did not show: not every measure moved, and the authors say so. People reported on themselves, and the records ran for weeks, not years.

    Emmons, R. A., & McCullough, M. E. (2003). Counting blessings versus burdens: An experimental investigation of gratitude and subjective well-being in daily life. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 84(2), 377–389. doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.84.2.377

    A real effect, and a small one

    A set of meta-analyses pooled the trials of gratitude exercises. Against doing nothing but the measurements, gratitude practice improved psychological well-being by a small amount. Against another activity of the same kind the gain was smaller. Against an exercise that was itself psychologically active, gratitude did as well, and no better.

    What it did not show: that gratitude practice lowers anxiety. In the five comparisons that measured it, it did not clearly do so.

    Davis, D. E., Choe, E., Meyers, J., Wade, N., Varjas, K., Gifford, A., Quinn, A., Hook, J. N., Van Tongeren, D. R., Griffin, B. J., & Worthington, E. L., Jr. (2016). Thankful for the little things: A meta-analysis of gratitude interventions. Journal of Counseling Psychology, 63(1), 20–31. doi.org/10.1037/cou0000107

    Gratitude and the thoughts before sleep

    In a questionnaire study of 401 adults, the more grateful people reported better and longer sleep, less time lying awake before it, and less trouble the next day. The link ran through what was in their minds as they fell asleep: more positive thoughts and fewer negative ones. It held after personality was taken into account.

    What it did not show: that asking the questions tonight will make you sleep. This was one questionnaire at one moment: it shows that grateful people sleep better, not why.

    Wood, A. M., Joseph, S., Lloyd, J., & Atkins, S. (2009). Gratitude influences sleep through the mechanism of pre-sleep cognitions. Journal of Psychosomatic Research, 66(1), 43–48. doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychores.2008.09.002

    Naikan, where the questions come from

    Naikan is a Japanese method of structured self-reflection, and the book names it as one root of this tool. In one recent study, sixty people took Naikan sessions over five days. Saliva samples showed oxytocin rising and the stress hormone cortisol falling after the sessions.

    What it did not show: the abstract reports no comparison group, so the change cannot be pinned on Naikan itself. And five days of guided sessions is far more than fifteen minutes with a journal.

    Qian, M., Wang, M., Song, S., Xia, H., Huang, R., Yuan, Q., Zhu, Z., Wei, H., Chen, M., Ma, Q., & Zhang, H. (2025). Investigating the psychophysiological effects of NaiKan Therapy: Salivary oxytocin and cortisol release. Frontiers in Integrative Neuroscience, 19, 1476654. doi.org/10.3389/fnint.2025.1476654

    Where the Three Questions sit in this

    The first question is the one the trials tested: writing down, specifically, what you received. The evidence for it is real and modest.

    The second and third questions, what you gave and what trouble you caused, are the weight of Ona's accounting. No study here tested the three together as a page written at night. That the third question clears the ledger, so that resentment does not build, is the teaching's own claim and the plain sense of the question. It is not a measured result.

    Ona did not keep this practice to feel better. She kept it “So nothing could convince me I had nothing.” That use of it, holding on to what is yours when much has been taken, is older than any of these papers.

    This is a practice, not medical care. The third question is for clearing, not for shame. If it turns into a list of reasons to punish yourself, stop there and take the three breaths.

    Three questions to think on or write about

    1. Vytautas wrote about the weather for fifty years because the weather could not be censored. What do you say to the people you love when you cannot say the thing itself? Do they hear it?
    2. “I did not feel left behind. I felt put somewhere on purpose.” Is there a place in your own story where that sentence could be true?
    3. Ona's theory is that he could not come back and find it changed: “That as long as he did not look, it was still there.” What have you not gone back to look at?
