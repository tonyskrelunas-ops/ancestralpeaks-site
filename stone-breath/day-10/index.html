    Today's challenge · Tool 10 of 22

    The Envy Protocol

    Today, do not send the third arrow. Feel the sting, name it, breathe with the stone, and go back to your work.

    The tool, as the book gives it

    Origin: Navajo (Masan’s teaching) + Japanese (Naikan & Third Arrow) + Lithuanian (Rye Field).

    Do not send the third arrow. The first arrow is what someone does to you. The second arrow is your reaction. The third arrow is what you do because of your reaction. Feel the sting. Name it. Then pause. Do not act from the reaction.

    Ask the three Naikan questions about the person who is hurting you:
    – What have I received from them?
    – What have I given to them?
    – What troubles have I caused them?

    Become the rye. The rye field doesn’t care what grows next to it. It just grows. Your growth does not diminish theirs. Theirs does not diminish yours.

    Use the stone. Breathe. In four, hold four, out six, hold four. Five times. Then get back to work.

    Keep building. Build so well, so consistently, that eventually there’s nothing left for the reducing to do.

    For the reader. You are not paranoid. Some people genuinely want to reduce you. Not because you did anything wrong – because your existence reminds them of what they lack. That is their problem, not yours. Your only job is to stay on your path and flourish.

    From “The Complete Toolkit” in Stone Breath.

    Study · Day 10

    Replaying the wound: what the research found, and what it did not

    The teaching about the arrows is far older than any laboratory. Here is what has been measured since about replaying a hurt, about envy itself, and about Naikan, in plain words, with the papers so you can read them yourself.

    Replaying the hurt keeps it alive

    A large review of the research on rumination, the habit of going over and over what is wrong, found good support for this much: it makes low mood worse, deepens negative thinking, gets in the way of solving the problem and of acting, and wears down the support of other people.

    What it did not show: this is a review of rumination and depression in general, not of envy or of being talked about. It also found that rumination predicts the start of a depression more reliably than how long it lasts.

    Nolen-Hoeksema, S., Wisco, B. E., & Lyubomirsky, S. (2008). Rethinking rumination. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 3(5), 400–424. doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x

    A step back changes what the replaying does

    In two experiments, people called back a time someone had made them angry. Those who looked at it from a distance, and asked why they felt as they did, could think it through without the feeling heating up again. Those who went back inside it could not.

    What it did not show: these were recall exercises in a laboratory, done once. They are not weeks of living next to people who wish you smaller.

    Kross, E., Ayduk, O., & Mischel, W. (2005). When asking “why” does not hurt: Distinguishing rumination from reflective processing of negative emotions. Psychological Science, 16(9), 709–715. doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2005.01600.x

    There are two kinds of envy

    Studies in the Netherlands, the United States and Spain found two different experiences under the one word. Benign envy moves a person to lift their own position. Malicious envy moves a person to pull the other one down.

    What it did not show: the studies describe what the envious person feels. They did not test what the envied person should do. The rye field is the book’s answer to that, not the study’s.

    van de Ven, N., Zeelenberg, M., & Pieters, R. (2009). Leveling up and down: The experiences of benign and malicious envy. Emotion, 9(3), 419–429. doi.org/10.1037/a0015669

    Naikan, in one small clinical study

    In Japan, 47 patients with major depression completed a week of intensive Naikan. Afterward 24 kept up a daily Naikan practice and 23 did not. Over the next three months the two groups differed in how their depression, anxiety and bodily complaints changed, and the authors conclude that daily practice helped hold the gains.

    What it did not show: it is one small study of patients in treatment, and its summary does not describe the two groups as chosen by chance. It did not test the three questions asked about a person who envies you.

    Sengoku, M., Murata, H., Kawahara, T., Imamura, K., & Nakagome, K. (2010). Does daily Naikan therapy maintain the efficacy of intensive Naikan therapy against depression? Psychiatry and Clinical Neurosciences, 64(1), 44–51. doi.org/10.1111/j.1440-1819.2009.02049.x

    Where the Envy Protocol sits in this

    The Envy Protocol puts a pause between the second arrow and the third: feel it, name it, breathe, go back to the work. The studies above fit that shape. Replaying feeds the wound, a step back cools it, and the envy that pulls down is a real and separate thing.

    No study here tested the Envy Protocol itself, the three arrows as a method, or the rye field. The book’s toolkit also names prisons and family relationships for Naikan; those studies were not found and checked for this page, so they are not claimed here.

    This is a practice, not medical care. If someone is harassing or threatening you, that is not a thing to breathe through alone: tell someone who can act.

    The passage to keep

    “You don’t stop the feelings. You just stop acting on them. You feel the envy, the fear, the shame – and you breathe. You hold the stone. You say: This is not mine to carry. Then you get back on the court.”

    “The envious ones try to reduce you. The answer is never to argue. The answer is to keep building. Build so well, so consistently, that eventually there’s nothing left for the reducing to do.”

    Tonas to Gintarė. Stone Breath, Chapter 10.

    Three questions to think on or write about

    1. Think of one first arrow from this month. What was your second arrow, and did you send a third?
    2. Ona says the shrinking buys you no peace. Where have you made yourself smaller so that someone else could be comfortable, and what did it cost?
    3. Ask the three Naikan questions about one person who has been hard on you: what you received, what you gave, what troubles you caused. Which answer surprised you?
