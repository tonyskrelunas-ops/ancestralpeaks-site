    Today's challenge · Tool 16 of 22

    The Passing

    Choose one thing you carry and one person who is ready. Tell them where it came from.

    The tool, as the book gives it

    Origin: Navajo lineage tradition.
    Lineage: Keh Yazh → Masan → Tonas → Ugnė.

    Choose what to pass. A stone, a piece of jewelry, a book, a song, a practice. What holds a story that must continue?

    Choose whom. Not next in line – the one who is ready. Watch who asks questions. Who listens. Who already carries it without knowing it.

    Speak. Tell them what it is. Where it came from. Why it matters. Tell them why you chose them. What you see in them. Why you trust them.

    Pass. Hand it over. Let them hold it. Silence. Let them feel it.

    Release. It is theirs now. Not yours. Let them carry it their way. You can watch. You can smile. You can remember. But you don’t get to control it anymore.

    You don’t need a fortune. You don’t need fame. You need one person who will carry what you gave them. A stone. A song. A story. A way of breathing when the world gets loud.

    From “The Complete Toolkit” (Tool 16) in Stone Breath.

    Study · Day 20

    What the research found, and what it did not

    Masan calls it a covenant. Researchers call it generativity: care for the ones who come after you. Here is what has been measured, with the papers so you can read them yourself.

    A name for it, and a way to measure it

    This paper set out a theory of generativity in seven linked parts, among them the inner desire, the concern for the next generation, the commitment, the acts, and the story a person tells about it. It gave researchers a scale that held up on retest and went together with real acts, such as teaching someone a skill.

    What it did not show: it is a theory and a measuring tool. It makes no claim yet about health or a long life.

    McAdams, D. P., & de St. Aubin, E. (1992). A theory of generativity and its assessment through self-report, behavioral acts, and narrative themes in autobiography. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 62(6), 1003–1015. doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.62.6.1003

    Feeling useful, ten years on

    In a national study in the United States, adults aged 60 to 75 who felt more generative, and who gave more to others, were somewhat less likely to lose the ability to manage daily tasks, or to die, over the next ten years. That held after their starting health was taken into account.

    What it did not show: it watched people; it did not assign anything. People in better shape may also find it easier to feel useful, and the differences were modest.

    Gruenewald, T. L., Liao, D. H., & Seeman, T. E. (2012). Contributing to others, contributing to oneself: Perceptions of generativity and health in later life. The Journals of Gerontology: Series B, 67(6), 660–665. doi.org/10.1093/geronb/gbs034

    Can it be grown?

    In the Baltimore Experience Corps Trial, older adults were assigned at random either to a program built on working with the young or to the usual volunteer opportunities. Those in the program reported more desire to be generative and more sense of having been so at four months, at a year and at two years, and more time in the program gave a larger effect.

    What it did not show: it measured the feeling of handing something on, not health.

    Gruenewald, T. L., Tanner, E. K., Fried, L. P., Carlson, M. C., Xue, Q.-L., Parisi, J. M., Rebok, G. W., Yarnell, L. M., & Seeman, T. E. (2016). The Baltimore Experience Corps Trial: Enhancing generativity via intergenerational activity engagement in later life. The Journals of Gerontology: Series B, 71(4), 661–670. doi.org/10.1093/geronb/gbv005

    Six weeks of writing it down

    Seventy-three women aged 60 to 86 were assigned at random to write for six weeks either about their life experience and their advice for others, or about neutral things. The first group took more part in social activities, reported less distress and better expectations of aging, and showed less activity in genes tied to inflammation.

    What it did not show: the authors call it preliminary: a small study, women only, six weeks.

    Moieni, M., Irwin, M. R., Seeman, T. E., Robles, T. F., Lieberman, M. D., Breen, E. C., Okimoto, S., Lengacher, C., Arevalo, J. M. G., Olmstead, R., Cole, S. W., & Eisenberger, N. I. (2020). Feeling needed: Effects of a randomized generativity intervention on well-being and inflammation in older women. Brain, Behavior, and Immunity, 84, 97–105. doi.org/10.1016/j.bbi.2019.11.014

    Where the Passing sits in this

    The research supports the heart of it: feeling that you have something to hand on goes with better later years, and that feeling can be grown by doing it.

    The toolkit says the act of passing “activates the same neural circuits as caregiving” and “releases oxytocin.” No study here measured that, and none tested a passing like this one. Read those two sentences as the book’s explanation. The five steps, and the letting go that Ona says will cost you, belong to Masan and Ona, not to a laboratory.

    This is a practice, not medical care.

    The passage to keep

    “A stone you keep is only a rock. A stone you pass is a covenant.”

    “What you pass on stops being yours. It has to. A song held too tightly in one throat dies in that throat.”

    Masan, then Ona. Stone Breath, Chapter 20.

    Three questions to think on or write about

    1. What do you carry that “holds a story that must not end with you”?
    2. Who is “already carrying it without knowing”? What have you seen them do?
    3. Ona: “You may watch. You may smile. You do not get to steer.” What would be hardest for you to stop steering?
