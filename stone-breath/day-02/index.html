    Today's challenge · Tool 1 of 22, taken all the way

    The Stone Breath: The Whole Practice

    Do the whole practice once before the day starts: the stream, your pebble, five rounds, the thanks. That is the whole challenge for today.

    The tool, as the book gives it

    As the founders taught it, for one voice to guide another, or for you to walk alone.

    Read this slowly, aloud if you are guiding another, and leave silence between the beats. The silence is part of the practice. It cannot be rushed and it cannot be skipped, and it takes only a handful of minutes to change a whole day.

    One thing before you begin: the stone never goes in your mouth. It stays in your hand. The tongue is imagination only. That is the whole safety rule and there are no exceptions to it.

    You are standing in the mountains, beside a stream. Let the place build itself around you — do not force it, let it come.

    Now kneel at the stream and put your hand in the water. Feel the cold of it, the aliveness of it. Cup the water in your hand. Lift it, and sprinkle it back — and as you do, offer gratitude.

    Now look into the water and find your pebble. Not any pebble — the one that calls to you. You will know it. Pick it up. Hold it, and think about its journey.

    Now — keep it in your hand, and imagine it resting on your tongue. Only imagine.

    Breathe in — four counts. Hold — four. Breathe out, slow — six. Hold — four. Five rounds. Take your time. There is nowhere to be. This is the place you were going.

    And now think of the ancestors. All of them, the whole long line that ends in you standing here.

    Now, gently, visualize setting the pebble back down in the stream — back into the water that shaped it. Offer thanks. Open your eyes. Relax. Begin your day.

    For the run (Tool 1b, the Stone Breath Protocol): On the first hill, lean from your ankles, not your waist. Match your breath to your steps. Soften your eyes to the horizon. And do not look back.

    Study · Day 2

    Posture, the stride and the wide gaze: what has been measured

    Today the breath is given a body: one line from heel to crown, a breath tied to the steps, eyes on the horizon. Here is what researchers have found about each, in plain words, and where the teaching goes further than the laboratory has.

    A slumped chest moves less air

    Seventy able-bodied volunteers were measured in four postures: sitting normally, sitting slumped, sitting with added lower-back support, and standing. Every measure of lung capacity and outward airflow was better standing than in normal or slumped sitting, and slumping lowered them further.

    What it did not show: these were hard-breath tests on people holding still. Nobody was running, and nobody was leaning from the ankles up a hill.

    Lin, F., Parthasarathy, S., Taylor, S. J., Pucci, D., Hendrix, R. W., & Makhsous, M. (2006). Effect of different sitting postures on lung capacity, expiratory flow, and lumbar lordosis. Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation, 87(4), 504–509. doi.org/10.1016/j.apmr.2005.11.031

    Slouching weakens the breath in

    Thirty-five healthy men aged 18 to 35 took a sniff test of breathing-muscle strength, once sitting upright and once slouched. The slouched score was lower, by 8.7 cmH2O on average. The authors read this as a diaphragm with less tension and less room to move.

    What it did not show: one small study of young men, sitting. A lower score on a strength test is not the same thing as running short of air on a climb.

    Albarrati, A., Zafar, H., Alghadir, A. H., & Anwer, S. (2018). Effect of upright and slouched sitting postures on the respiratory muscle strength in healthy young males. BioMed Research International, 2018, 3058970. doi.org/10.1155/2018/3058970

    Runners lock the breath to the stride

    Recordings of running hares, dogs, horses and people showed breathing locked in step with the limbs. Four-legged runners take one breath to a stride. People use several patterns, and two strides to a breath appeared to be the favored one.

    What it did not show: that locking the breath on purpose makes anyone faster or calmer. The paper describes what running bodies do; it does not test a way of training.

    Bramble, D. M., & Carrier, D. R. (1983). Running and breathing in mammals. Science, 219(4582), 251–256. doi.org/10.1126/science.6849136

    The body chooses the pattern that helps the breath

    Fourteen people ran at a pace they chose while their breathing and their steps were measured. Each footfall gives the air a small push. The runners settled into patterns where those pushes worked with the breath, and a breath that turned over at one of the preferred moments in the step did so twice as fast.

    What it did not show: fourteen runners, one easy pace. That the body does this to protect the breathing muscles from tiring is offered by the authors as a hypothesis.

    Daley, M. A., Bramble, D. M., & Carrier, D. R. (2013). Impact loading and locomotor-respiratory coordination significantly influence breathing dynamics in running humans. PLoS ONE, 8(8), e70752. doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0070752

    Where the whole practice sits in this

    The book's protocol holds the body in one long line from heel to crown so the chest stays open, ties the breath to the steps, and softens the eyes to take in the whole land. The posture studies fit the first of these. For the second: if a pace is one footfall, in for two and out for two comes to two strides for each breath, the pattern Bramble and Carrier saw most often in people.

    The wide, soft gaze has a name in meditation research: open monitoring, attention that rests on the whole field without holding on to one object. The paper below describes that style and how it may train attention and emotion. It does not test runners. What the gaze does for fear and for heavy legs comes from the runners and their teachers, not from a laboratory.

    No study here tested a stone in the hand. The stream, the pebble's long journey and the thanks are the founders' way of walking you there; they have not been measured, and they do not need to be to be done well.

    Lutz, A., Slagter, H. A., Dunne, J. D., & Davidson, R. J. (2008). Attention regulation and monitoring in meditation. Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 12(4), 163–169. doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2008.01.005

    This is a practice, not medical care. If holding the breath makes you dizzy or uneasy, leave the holds out and keep the long breath out. On a run the breath keeps moving; it is never held.

    The passage to keep

    “My father ran with a stone in his hand, imagining it on his tongue. Not a big one. Small. Smooth. The kind the river leaves on its banks for free.”

    “He gave it to me. I give it to you. That is how this works.”

    Masan, at the end of the chapter. Stone Breath, Chapter 2.

    Three questions to think on or write about

    1. Tonas knew the tools “the way you know the words of a song you no longer feel.” Which practice of your own has become only a technique? What would it take to feel it again?
    2. “He never felt abandoned. He felt placed.” Where were you placed, and by whom? What did they put in your hands there?
    3. Before he left, Tonas told the land he would carry it “Not as weight. As witness.” What are you carrying as weight that could be carried as witness?
