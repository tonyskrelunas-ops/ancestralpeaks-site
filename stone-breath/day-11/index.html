    Today's challenge · Tool 11 of 22

    The Community Kitchen Model

    Ask one person the four questions: what do you love, what is hurting, what do you have, and what is the smallest thing we could finish in six months? Then listen all the way to the end of the answer.

    The tool, as the book gives it

    Origin: Adapted from Lithuanian talka + Japanese nemawashi + Navajo community organizing.

    Ask the four questions. What do you love? What is hurting? What do you have? What’s the smallest thing you can finish in six months?

    Talk to everyone alone first. In Japan, they call this nemawashi – preparing the roots before you transplant the tree.

    Sit in a circle. No head of the table. A stone in the center.

    Start with one night a week. A community dinner, cooked by volunteers, using food from local gardens.

    Add a market table. A place for people to sell what they make.

    Start a communal garden. Anyone can plant. Anyone can take. The kitchen buys what’s left.

    Find a leader. Someone who cooks. Someone who cares. Someone the community trusts.

    Find the money. One page. Four paragraphs. The truth.

    For the reader. Tonas didn’t invent the community kitchen. He just asked the four questions and listened to the answers. The answer was already there. It always is.

    From “The Complete Toolkit” in Stone Breath.

    Five rounds with the stone before you ask anyone anything. Then the four questions.

    Study · Day 11

    Eating together: what the research found, and what it did not

    Ona did not need a study to know that a village eats together. Here is what has been measured since, in plain words, with the papers so you can read them yourself.

    People who eat with others more often

    A national survey in the United Kingdom found that people who eat with others more often feel happier and more satisfied with life, trust others more, are more engaged with their local communities, and have more friends they can depend on. The meals that left people feeling closer had more people, more laughter and more reminiscing.

    What it did not show: it is a survey taken at one time. The author’s analysis suggests the direction runs from eating together to feeling bonded; a survey can suggest that, not prove it.

    Dunbar, R. I. M. (2017). Breaking bread: The functions of social eating. Adaptive Human Behavior and Physiology, 3(3), 198–211. doi.org/10.1007/s40750-017-0061-4

    Living with family, eating alone

    Among 1,856 older adults living at home in Kashiwa, Japan, those who lived with their families and still ate alone were at particular risk of depressive symptoms: about five times the odds for those in the younger part of old age, and more than twice the odds for the oldest.

    What it did not show: everyone was measured once, so the study cannot say whether eating alone brings the low mood or the low mood empties the chair.

    Kuroda, A., Tanaka, T., Hirano, H., Ohara, Y., Kikutani, T., Furuya, H., Obuchi, S. P., Kawai, H., Ishii, S., Akishita, M., Tsuji, T., & Iijima, K. (2015). Eating alone as social disengagement is strongly associated with depressive symptoms in Japanese community-dwelling older adults. Journal of the American Medical Directors Association, 16(7), 578–585. doi.org/10.1016/j.jamda.2015.01.078

    The family table and the young

    A systematic review searched 1,783 articles and kept the 14 that met its bar. Across them, frequent family meals went with less disordered eating, less alcohol and substance use, less violent behaviour, and fewer feelings of depression or thoughts of suicide in adolescents.

    What it did not show: these are associations. Families who eat together often differ in other ways too, and the review cannot separate the meal from the family around it.

    Harrison, M. E., Norris, M. L., Obeid, N., Fu, M., Weinstangel, H., & Sampson, M. (2015). Systematic review of the effects of family meal frequency on psychosocial outcomes in youth. Canadian Family Physician, 61(2), e96–e106. europepmc.org/article/MED/25676655

    Belonging and how long people live

    A review of 148 studies with 308,849 people found that those with stronger social relationships were 50 percent more likely to be alive at follow-up. The link was strongest where the measure was fullest: how woven into a community a person was.

    What it did not show: it is about relationships of every kind, not about meals, and it follows people rather than assigning them a village.

    Holt-Lunstad, J., Smith, T. B., & Layton, J. B. (2010). Social relationships and mortality risk: A meta-analytic review. PLoS Medicine, 7(7), e1000316. doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316

    Where the Community Kitchen sits in this

    The model is four questions, a visit to each person alone, a circle with no head of the table, and one shared meal a week. The research above speaks most directly to the last part: people who eat together are, on the whole, closer, more trusting and less alone.

    No study here tested the four questions, nemawashi, or a village café. The book’s line that small, consistent gatherings build trust faster than any strategic plan is the experience of people who have organized; it is offered as that, not as a measured result.

    A shared table is not medical care. If a low mood will not lift, tell a doctor or someone you trust, and come to the table too.

    The passage to keep

    “You want to know how a village stays alive? It eats together.”

    “My grandmother told me: a fire that is never fed goes out. A community that never gathers is not a community. It is just people living near each other, waiting for something to go wrong before they speak.”

    Ona. Stone Breath, Chapter 11.

    Three questions to think on or write about

    1. Answer the four questions for one place you belong to: what do you love, what is hurting, what do you have, and what is the smallest thing you could finish in six months?
    2. Tonas says: “You can’t skip the messy part. The messy part is the work.” Where are you trying to skip it?
    3. Old Jonas raised his glass “because someone finally asked what he loved.” Who near you has not been asked in a long time?
