    Today's challenge · Tool 8 of 22

    The Compatibility Lens

    Watch how one person treats someone who can do nothing for them. Then ask it of yourself: welcome, lightness, honor.

    The tool, as the book gives it

    Origin: Navajo Woshdee/Bahozho/K’enidzin + Japanese ba + Yorùbá àṣẹ.

    Woshdee (welcome): Do they offer food and genuine warmth? Or do they hold back, measure you, wait to see what you can do for them?

    Bahozho (happiness): Do they carry lightness? Laugh easily? Or do they drain the energy from every room they enter?

    K’enidzin (honor): Do they lift others up? Celebrate your wins without jealousy? Or do they cut communication, relay gossip, get frantic when you succeed?

    Watch how they treat people who can do nothing for them – the waiter, the old woman at the post office, the annoying child. That’s who they actually are.

    Trust day three: if you feel smaller instead of larger after three encounters, that is information. Act on it.

    The Frybread Test: Can they do something hard (make frybread) and tell the truth (the Long Walk story) at the same time? If they need silence to lie, do not trust them.

    The Compatibility Lens is not about judging others. It’s about protecting your energy so you can keep building.

    Study · Day 17

    What the research found, and what it did not

    Of Masan’s three questions, researchers have looked hardest at the third: what a person does when something good happens to you. They call the telling of good news capitalization, and they have watched closely what the answer does. Here it is in plain words, with the papers so you can read them yourself.

    Good news, told

    Four studies. Telling others about a good event went with more positive feeling and well-being that day, over and above the event itself. When the listener answered actively and constructively, the benefit grew. And where a partner usually answered good news with enthusiasm, the relationship had more intimacy and more daily satisfaction.

    What it did not show: cause. These are diary and questionnaire studies; they show what goes together.

    Gable, S. L., Reis, H. T., Impett, E. A., & Asher, E. R. (2004). What do you do when things go right? The intrapersonal and interpersonal benefits of sharing positive events. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 87(2), 228–245. doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.87.2.228

    Being there when things go right

    In this study, 79 dating couples were filmed talking over recent good events and bad ones. Two months later, the way a partner had answered the good news was more closely tied to how well the relationship was doing, and to whether the couple had broken up, than the way they had answered the bad.

    What it did not show: anything beyond dating couples and two months. It is about partners, not every friend or colleague.

    Gable, S. L., Gonzaga, G. C., & Strachman, A. (2006). Will you be there for me when things go right? Supportive responses to positive event disclosures. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 91(5), 904–917. doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.91.5.904

    Enthusiasm builds trust, even between strangers

    Four experiments and a daily diary study. Sharing good news raised its value to the teller, most of all when the listener was enthusiastic. And an enthusiastic answer built trust in the listener and a giving attitude toward them, between strangers in a laboratory as well as in close relationships.

    What it did not show: what happens over years. These are short encounters and daily reports.

    Reis, H. T., Smith, S. M., Carmichael, C. L., Caprariello, P. A., Tsai, F.-F., Rodrigues, A., & Maniaci, M. R. (2010). Are you happy for me? How sharing positive events with others provides personal and interpersonal benefits. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 99(2), 311–329. doi.org/10.1037/a0018344

    The people who bring out who you mean to be

    Four studies described what the authors named the Michelangelo phenomenon. When a partner sees you and treats you in line with the person you are trying to become, you move toward that person, and the relationship works better and lasts.

    What it did not show: more than an association. The measures are people’s own reports about their partners, and the studies are of couples.

    Drigotas, S. M., Rusbult, C. E., Wieselquist, J., & Whitton, S. W. (1999). Close partner as sculptor of the ideal self: Behavioral affirmation and the Michelangelo phenomenon. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 77(2), 293–323. doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.77.2.293

    Where the Compatibility Lens sits in this

    Masan’s third question is the one these papers come closest to: “When something good happens to you — a win, a recognition, a joy — do they move toward it? Or do they get quiet?” The research on good news says the answer matters, for trust and for whether a bond lasts. And the tool’s plain measure, whether you feel smaller or larger after three encounters, is near to what the fourth paper describes.

    No study here tested the three pillars as a set, the rule of day three, or the frybread test. Watching how a person treats the ones who can do nothing for them comes from Masan, and from Ona’s talka, not from a laboratory. The book is careful about how it is to be used: “This is not judgment.”

    This is a practice, not medical care, and not a verdict on anyone.

    The passage to keep

    “You want to know how I read a person? I watch how they treat the ones who cannot help them. The child who is boring. The old one who moves slowly. The visitor who has nothing to offer. That is who they actually are. Everything else is performance.”

    “That is your answer. Not what they say about themselves when you ask. What they do when there is something heavy to carry and no one is keeping score.”

    Masan, then Ona. Stone Breath, Chapter 17.

    Three questions to think on or write about

    1. Who moved toward your last piece of good news, and who got quiet? What did you do with what you saw?
    2. Her father asks, “So. Are you a pine or a birch?” and says of his own trees, “Thirty-one years I have worked for a thing I will not see.” What are you working on that will not finish inside your life?
    3. Turn the lens around. This week, how did you treat the person who could do nothing for you?
