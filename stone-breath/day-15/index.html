    Today's challenge · Tool 7 of 22

    The Land Covenant

    Choose your piece of land: a tree, a stretch of river, a corner of a park. Go there today, and give one small thing back.

    The tool, as the book gives it

    Origin: Navajo – Masan’s morning prayer / Lithuanian – Ona’s forest walks.

    Find your piece of land. Not a random spot – a specific tree, a stretch of river, a view from a hill, a corner of a park, a garden bed. A place you can return to.

    Learn it in every season. Visit in spring when the snow melts. In summer when the leaves are full. In autumn when they fall. In winter when the branches are bare. Notice what changes. Notice what stays the same.

    Give back to it. Pick up what doesn’t belong. Plant something native. Bring water during a drought. Pull an invasive weed. Small acts, repeated, build the covenant.

    Take a child there. Tell them its name. Tell them a story about it – your story, or a story from your people.

    Make a promise. Not to the land – to yourself. I will not forget this place. I will not take more than I need. I will leave it better than I found it.

    You don’t need to own land to make a covenant. You just need to show up. Again and again.

    From “The Complete Toolkit” in Stone Breath.

    Study · Day 15

    What the research found, and what it did not

    Masan and Ona went back to the same ground every morning without needing a study. Here is what has been measured since about time outdoors, growing up near green, and giving back, in plain words, with the papers so you can read them yourself.

    Twenty minutes, in a place you chose

    For eight weeks, 36 city dwellers took a “nature experience” at least three times a week, for ten minutes or more, at a time and place of their own choosing, and gave saliva before and after. Cortisol fell 21.3 percent an hour beyond its ordinary daily drop, and the return for the time spent was greatest between twenty and thirty minutes.

    What it did not show: anything about going back to one same place over years. There were 36 people, and no group that stayed indoors to compare them with.

    Hunter, M. R., Gillespie, B. W., & Chen, S. Y. (2019). Urban nature experiences reduce stress in the context of daily life based on salivary biomarkers. Frontiers in Psychology, 10, 722. doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00722

    Across 143 studies

    A systematic review and meta-analysis brought together 103 observational and 40 interventional studies. More exposure to greenspace went with lower salivary cortisol, lower heart rate and diastolic blood pressure, and higher high-frequency heart rate variability, and with more people reporting good health.

    What it did not show: cause. Most of the studies were observational, so they show what goes together. And greenspace is measured in general here, not as one known place.

    Twohig-Bennett, C., & Jones, A. (2018). The health benefits of the great outdoors: A systematic review and meta-analysis of greenspace exposure and health outcomes. Environmental Research, 166, 628–637. doi.org/10.1016/j.envres.2018.06.030

    Children who grow up near green

    A Danish study of about a million people used satellite data to measure the green space around each person’s home from birth to the age of ten. Those who grew up with the least green had up to 55 percent higher risk of a range of psychiatric disorders later in life than those with the most, even after adjusting for urbanization, socioeconomic factors, and the parents’ age and history of mental illness.

    What it did not show: that green space is the cause; it is an association found in records. And it measured green near the home, not a child’s bond with one particular tree or creek.

    Engemann, K., Pedersen, C. B., Arge, L., Tsirogiannis, C., Mortensen, P. B., & Svenning, J.-C. (2019). Residential green space in childhood is associated with lower risk of psychiatric disorders from adolescence into adulthood. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 116(11), 5188–5193. doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1807504116

    Giving back, twenty years on

    Using the Alameda County Study, which has followed a cohort of 6,928 adults since 1965, researchers asked whether volunteering for environmental work in midlife, in 1974, predicted health in 1994. It was associated with more physical activity, better self-reported health and fewer depressive symptoms.

    What it did not show: proof. The authors call it the first epidemiological evidence and ask for intervention studies to confirm it.

    Pillemer, K., Fuller-Rowell, T. E., Reid, M. C., & Wells, N. M. (2010). Environmental volunteering and health outcomes over a 20-year period. The Gerontologist, 50(5), 594–602. doi.org/10.1093/geront/gnq007

    Where the Land Covenant sits in this

    The book says: “Your nervous system regulates differently when you are on land that knows you.” The papers here stand behind part of that. Time in green places goes with lower cortisol and higher heart rate variability, children who grow up with green near home do better later, and volunteering for the land in midlife went with better health twenty years on.

    What no study here tested is the covenant itself: one particular place, returned to through the seasons, treated as a relative, and given back to. That part comes from Masan’s mornings and Ona’s forest, not from a laboratory. In the book’s words: “The covenant is not ownership. It is kinship.”

    This is a practice, not medical care.

    “Three questions. And you can make this covenant with any ground that holds you — a creek, a park, a patch of dirt behind your building. It travels.”

    Three questions to think on or write about

    1. Which piece of ground would know you if you came back to it tomorrow? When were you last there?
    2. Austėja had always known, with her mind, that the songs were not held in the walls. At the locked door, “This was the first time she knew it in her body.” What do you know with your mind that your body has not learned yet?
    3. Doris says of the grandfather who never went back: “He bought the tickets twice.” Whom have you judged as afraid, who may have been giving quietly somewhere you could not see?
