    Today's challenge · Tool 4 of 22

    The Ancestral Council

    Sit a few minutes. Choose one elder, ask one question, and wait.

    The tool, as the book gives it

    Origin: Navajo – Ada’s morning centering and Masan’s teaching that the ancestors are always present.

    Find a quiet place where you won’t be disturbed. Sit comfortably, spine long, feet on the floor if you’re in a chair. Hold a stone if you have one.

    Part 1: Arrival (2 minutes) – Close your eyes. Take three slow breaths – in through the nose, out through the nose. Feel the ground beneath you. Feel the air on your skin. Feel the weight of your body.

    Part 2: Choose Your Elder (1 minute) – Think of someone you look up to. A grandparent, teacher, mentor. Someone whose wisdom you carry – even if you forgot. It can be someone you knew, someone you never met, someone from history, someone from story.

    Part 3: The Visit (8 minutes) – See yourself approaching them. Where are you? Their kitchen? A garden? A forest? Silently ask them one question – something you’re struggling with. Don’t force the answer. Just wait.

    Part 4: Through Their Eyes (2 minutes) – Now look at yourself through their eyes. What do they notice about you? What do they love about you? What have you forgotten?

    Part 5: Thank & Return (2 minutes) – Thank them. Feel the stone. One breath together. Open your eyes.

    As Tonas gives it to Ugnė: “You close your eyes. You choose an elder – someone who came before. Someone whose wisdom you carry. You ask them one question. Then you wait. You don’t force the answer. You just wait.”

    Study · Day 8

    What the research found, and what it did not

    Tonas tells Ugnė: you ask one question, then you wait. Here is what has been studied about looking at your own trouble from one step outside it, and about calling to mind someone who holds you, with the papers so you can read them yourself.

    Wiser about other people’s trouble than our own

    In three experiments with 693 people, run by researchers at the University of Waterloo and the University of Michigan, people reasoned more wisely about someone else’s problem than about their own: they saw the limits of what they knew, looked for compromise, and took in other points of view. When they were asked to step back and look at their own problem from a distance, the gap closed. It closed for the young and the old alike.

    What it did not show: stepping back in that study was a change of viewpoint, not a visit with an elder. And the older adults were no wiser about their own conflicts than the younger ones: age alone did not do it.

    Grossmann, I., & Kross, E. (2014). Exploring Solomon’s paradox: Self-distancing eliminates the self-other asymmetry in wise reasoning about close relationships in younger and older adults. Psychological Science, 25(8), 1571–1580. doi.org/10.1177/0956797614535400

    A month of writing from one step back

    Two preregistered experiments followed 555 adults. For a month, some wrote each day about the day’s most significant event as “I,” and others wrote about themselves in the third person. Those who wrote from the outside showed a significant rise in wise reasoning about their conflicts with other people. A week-long study found the same.

    What it did not show: this was a written diary, not a sitting with the eyes closed. It measured how people reasoned, not whether their lives went better.

    Grossmann, I., Dorfman, A., Oakes, H., Santos, H. C., Vohs, K. D., & Scholer, A. A. (2021). Training for wisdom: The distanced-self-reflection diary method. Psychological Science, 32(3), 381–394. doi.org/10.1177/0956797620969170

    Asking why, without drowning in it

    In two experiments people called up a time someone had made them angry. Those who asked why they felt as they did while watching the scene from a distance could think it through without the anger flooding back. The authors call it cool, reflective processing, as against rumination.

    What it did not show: two laboratory studies of remembered anger. Not grief, not fear, and not a decision about a life.

    Kross, E., Ayduk, O., & Mischel, W. (2005). When asking “why” does not hurt: Distinguishing rumination from reflective processing of negative emotions. Psychological Science, 16(9), 709–715. doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2005.01600.x

    Calling to mind someone who holds you

    Across three studies, people recalled an upsetting memory and then brought to mind a person they were deeply attached to. They recovered from the memory better than people who thought of an acquaintance or a stranger. Bringing the person to mind before the memory did not help; after it, it did.

    What it did not show: the people called to mind were the participants’ own attachment figures. The studies did not test elders who have died, people from history, or people from story. The effect was weaker in people who keep others at a distance.

    Selcuk, E., Zayas, V., Günaydin, G., Hazan, C., & Kross, E. (2012). Mental representations of attachment figures facilitate recovery following upsetting autobiographical memory recall. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 103(2), 362–378. doi.org/10.1037/a0028125

    Where the Ancestral Council sits in this

    The toolkit names “work from the University of Waterloo” for self-distancing: that is the first two papers above. It also names Frontiers in Psychology for the line that imagining a supportive relationship “activates the same neural networks as a real one”. That sentence could not be matched to one paper for this page, so it is left as the book’s own; the fourth study is the nearest finding that was checked, and it measured how people felt, not their brains.

    No study here tested the Ancestral Council itself. That the ancestors are always present is Masan’s teaching. It is not offered here as a finding, and it does not need to be one for you to sit down and ask.

    This is a practice, not medical care. If the elder who comes brings grief with them, open your eyes, feel the stone, and stop there for today.

    Three questions to think on or write about

    1. Marija, at the dough: “You press like a man who is trying to finish. Bread is not finished. Bread is let go of.” What are you pressing to finish that needs to be let go of?
    2. Old Jonas tells about a horse, and Tonas understands he has just been told about the war. Who in your life tells the large thing sideways? What have they been telling you?
    3. “Because if you know the forest, you are never poor,” Rytis says. What do you know so well that it would feed people through a hard winter? Who taught it to you?
