    Today's challenge · Tool 21 of 22

    The Engine Builder

    When the doubt rises, name it flat, like a reading on a gauge. Recall what you have built. Then take the next step.

    The tool, as the book gives it

    Origin: Tonas’s Lithuanian grandfather, Vytautas, was a master machinist and a champion hill climber — racing custom motorcycles he built with his own hands up near-vertical slopes, into his seventies. He understood what most performers never learn: you do not control the race. You control the machine you bring to it. Fear, doubt, envy — these are not enemies to defeat. They are readings on a gauge.

    Step 1: Read the Gauge. When the doubt or fear or envy rises, do not fight it. Name it, flat, like a reading: “That’s fear of the mistake.” “That’s the imposter voice.” “That’s comparison.” Naming it drops it from a storm to a number.

    Step 2: Check the Build. Every champion has a record. Recall it, specifically — the hours logged, the times you were tested and held. “I built this machine. Here is the proof.” Doubt is a feeling. Your preparation is a fact. Facts win.

    Step 3: Trust the Machine. You cannot control the other competitors, the weather, the outcome. You control the machine you bring — your breath, your preparation, your next single action. Put all your attention there.

    Step 4: Show Up and Run Your Line. One slow breath in through the nose. One long breath out. Then commit — fully, without hesitation.

    Why it is here today. The book gives this tool no chapter of its own. In this chapter the stopwatch comes out of Tonas’s pocket, with what Vytautas wrote to him: “When I was afraid, I would start the stopwatch. I would listen to the tick. It reminded me that time does not wait for courage. You must move before you are ready.”

    From “The Complete Toolkit” and Chapter 9 of Stone Breath.

    The tool asks for one slow breath in and one long breath out. The five rounds are for the days the gauge reads high.

    Study · Day 9

    What the research found, and what it did not

    Vytautas read a dial and did not panic at the reading. Here is what has been measured since about putting a feeling into plain words, and about what people do with the surge before a hard thing, with the papers so you can read them yourself.

    Naming the feeling quiets the alarm

    People lay in a brain scanner and looked at pictures carrying strong negative emotion. When they put the feeling into a word, the amygdala and other alarm regions of the brain answered less than when they handled the pictures in other ways, and one region at the front of the brain, on the right side, worked harder. The more that region worked, the quieter the amygdala.

    What it did not show: the feelings named were in pictures shown to them, not their own fear before a race. The study measured the brain’s response, not how anyone then performed.

    Lieberman, M. D., Eisenberger, N. I., Crockett, M. J., Tom, S. M., Pfeifer, J. H., & Way, B. M. (2007). Putting feelings into words: Affect labeling disrupts amygdala activity in response to affective stimuli. Psychological Science, 18(5), 421–428. doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x

    Saying the fear out loud, in front of the spider

    People afraid of spiders were brought close to a live one, again and again. Some named their fear in words as they did it; others were told to think of the spider differently, to distract themselves, or nothing. A week later, with a different spider in another place, the ones who had named the fear had a lower skin response, a measure of the body’s alarm, and came somewhat closer than the ones who had distracted themselves. The more fear words they had used, the more their fear response dropped.

    What it did not show: by their own report they were no less afraid than the other groups. The body was calmer; the feeling was still called fear.

    Kircanski, K., Lieberman, M. D., & Craske, M. G. (2012). Feelings into words: Contributions of language to exposure therapy. Psychological Science, 23(10), 1086–1091. doi.org/10.1177/0956797612443830

    The surge is not the enemy

    People preparing for a graduate entrance exam took a practice test. Half were told beforehand that the arousal they feel before a test improves performance. They did better on the math section than the others, and one to three months later they did better on the math section of the real exam too.

    What it did not show: one exam, and the gain was on the math section. Being told the surge helps is a cousin of reading the gauge, not the same thing.

    Jamieson, J. P., Mendes, W. B., Blackstock, E., & Schmader, T. (2010). Turning the knots in your stomach into bows: Reappraising arousal improves performance on the GRE. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 46(1), 208–212. doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2009.08.015

    Not calm. Ready.

    Across studies of karaoke singing, public speaking and math, most people believed the best thing to do with nerves was to calm down. The ones who instead named the feeling as excitement, as simply as saying “I am excited” out loud, felt more excited and performed better than the ones who tried to calm down.

    What it did not show: short tasks in a laboratory, each done once. And “excited” is a warmer word than the flat reading Vytautas would have given it.

    Brooks, A. W. (2014). Get excited: Reappraising pre-performance anxiety as excitement. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 143(3), 1144–1158. doi.org/10.1037/a0035325

    Where the Engine Builder sits in this

    The toolkit says: “Naming it drops it from a storm to a number.” The first two studies are the nearest evidence: a word for the feeling went with a quieter alarm in the brain and a calmer body. The last two say something the toolkit also says, that the point before a hard thing is not to be calm.

    No study here tested the four steps. Checking the build and trusting the machine have no paper on this page, and “This is how elite competitors stay calm where others come apart” is the book’s line, not a finding. The gauge, the build and the line come from a machinist who raced the hills into his seventies, and from a letter that came with a stopwatch.

    This is a practice, not medical care. If fear has been running your days for a long time, a person trained to help is part of a good build.

    The passage to keep

    “When I was afraid, I would start the stopwatch. I would listen to the tick. It reminded me that time does not wait for courage. You must move before you are ready.”

    “Later, he would call that night the moment everything shifted. Not because of the wine or the sunset or even the kiss on his cheek. Because he fell on his face — and she laughed with him instead of at him.”

    Vytautas, in the letter that came with the stopwatch; then the chapter’s close. Stone Breath, Chapter 9.

    Three questions to think on or write about

    1. Asked what the stopwatch measures, Tonas does not say time. He says courage. What are you waiting to feel ready for? What would moving first look like today?
    2. Masan had three questions for anybody: “Do they finish what they start. Do they feed people. Can they be happy for someone else’s good news.” Ask them of yourself.
    3. Ona: “The food is the smallest part.” Count her list: somebody says something first, somebody makes it by hand for people who did not pay, everyone sits down at the same time. Which part is missing from your table?
