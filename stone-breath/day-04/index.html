    Today's challenge · Tool 3 of 22

    The Daina Practice

    Find one song that holds something true for you. Sing it once today, out loud, for no one. That is the whole challenge for today.

    The tool, as the book gives it

    Origin: Lithuanian dainos – the living library of a people.

    Find a song that carries meaning for you. It doesn’t have to be ancient. It doesn’t have to be in another language. It just has to hold something true.

    It might be: a song your grandmother sang while she worked, a lullaby your mother hummed, a song that found you at the right moment, a melody that comes when you’re alone and quiet.

    Learn it. Not perfectly – just enough to carry it. Sing it. Not for anyone else. Sing it for yourself.

    Sing it to the trees. Sing it in the car. Sing it when you’re afraid.

    Teach it to one other person. The song does not survive in a recording. It survives in a throat.

    You don’t have to find the exact notes. Just find the practice. Sing something true.

    From “The Complete Toolkit” in Stone Breath.

    When

    Once today, out loud

    In the car, on a walk, at the sink. For no one. And if you are about to say that you do not sing, Ona has already answered: “That is not what I asked.”

    Study · Day 4

    Why a song holds: what the research found, and what it did not

    A daina crossed eighty years in a village's throats before Tonas heard it. The laboratory has only begun to look at what singing does to a body and to a group. Here is what has been measured, and how small some of the studies are.

    A song slows the breath, and the heart follows

    Healthy eighteen-year-olds hummed a single tone, sang a hymn breathing as they liked, and sang a slow mantra breathing only between the phrases, while their heart rate was recorded. The more regular the song, the more the singers' hearts sped up and slowed down together, in time with the long breaths the song asked for.

    What it did not show: that singing improves health over years. The authors discuss that as a possibility; the study measured minutes, in young singers.

    Vickhoff, B., Malmgren, H., Åström, R., Nyberg, G., Ekström, S.-R., Engwall, M., Snygg, J., Nilsson, M., & Jörnsten, R. (2013). Music structure determines heart rate variability of singers. Frontiers in Psychology, 4, 334. doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00334

    Singing together bonds strangers quickly

    Newly formed adult classes, some in singing and some in crafts or creative writing, were followed for seven months. By the third check everyone felt equally close to their group. But the singers got there much faster: their sense of closeness jumped in the first month, and the other classes caught up over time.

    What it did not show: that singing bonds people more deeply in the end. And closeness was what people said of themselves.

    Pearce, E., Launay, J., & Dunbar, R. I. M. (2015). The ice-breaker effect: Singing mediates fast social bonding. Royal Society Open Science, 2(10), 150221. doi.org/10.1098/rsos.150221

    Oxytocin: a first look, in four singers

    Four members of a vocal jazz group sang together twice, once from the page and once improvising, with blood drawn before and after. A stress hormone, ACTH, fell both times, significantly so in the composed singing. Oxytocin rose on average only after improvising, and the difference between the two kinds of singing was not significant.

    What it did not show: much, yet. Four people cannot settle anything, and the authors present it as a first test of the method. The toolkit names oxytocin and endorphins; on oxytocin, this is how early the evidence is.

    Keeler, J. R., Roth, E. A., Neuser, B. L., Spitsbergen, J. M., Waters, D. J. M., & Vianney, J.-M. (2015). The neurochemistry and social flow of singing: Bonding and oxytocin. Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 9, 518. doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00518

    Words that are sung are held differently

    People with Alzheimer's disease and healthy older adults studied the lyrics of children's songs they did not know, some heard sung and some spoken. Those with Alzheimer's recognized the sung lyrics better than the spoken ones. The healthy older adults showed no difference.

    What it did not show: that song beats speech for everyone's memory. Here it did so only where memory was failing, and the authors offer two possible reasons without choosing between them.

    Simmons-Stern, N. R., Budson, A. E., & Ally, B. A. (2010). Music as a memory enhancer in patients with Alzheimer's disease. Neuropsychologia, 48(10), 3164–3167. doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2010.04.033

    Where the Daina Practice sits in this

    Three of these four studies are about singing with other people. Today's challenge is one person and one song, sung for no one. What carries over is the breath: a song makes you breathe out long and slow, the same door the Stone Breath uses. The bonding waits for the last step of the tool, when you teach the song to one other person.

    No study here tested a daina, or a grandmother's song. What the chapter says about songs, that they survive in a throat and not in a recording, and that they can hold a name when the books are gone, comes from the people who kept them and not from a laboratory.

    This is a practice, not medical care. You do not need a good voice. Tonas sang badly, two beats behind, and Ona did not slow down for him even slightly.

    Three questions to think on or write about

    1. Whose voice do you hear when you are alone and quiet? What did they sing, or hum, while their hands were busy?
    2. The song Ona sings is the mother's side of a leaving: she waited at the edge of the forest every day. Who waited in your family, and was the waiting ever told?
    3. Austėja was told in Kyoto that “broken things become beautiful if you repair them with gold.” What in your own line was broken and is still waiting for the gold?
