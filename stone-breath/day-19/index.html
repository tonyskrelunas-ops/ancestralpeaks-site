    The Museum of Proof and Seijaku

    Start your museum: write three hard things you have already come through. Then rest on purpose before the next hard thing.

    The tools, as the book gives them

    Tool 14 · The Museum of Proof. Origin: Masan’s practice – speaking her survived hardships aloud. Tonas’s notebook.

    Create a museum – a notebook, a digital folder, a shoebox. Collect proof: every hard thing you have overcome. Every time you wanted to quit and didn’t. Every time someone said you couldn’t and you did. Write it down. Date it. Be specific.

    “Ran 15:03 5k. Legs burned. Kept going.”
    “Lost the semifinals by two seconds. Wanted to quit. Didn’t.”

    Visit the museum when doubt attacks – after a critical article, before a hard meeting, at 3 a.m. Read the evidence. The conclusion is always the same: you have done hard things before. You can do this one.

    Tool 22 · Seijaku, rest and stillness before the strike.

    1) Name the harvest — what you have been spending. 2) Lay it fallow on purpose — choose the rest before your body forces it; make it sacred, not stolen. 3) Make it active calm, not collapse — sit, breathe, let the mind go quiet. 4) Remember what it is for — hold the picture of who you are resting for. 5) Return restored, not depleted.

    The Stillness Before the Storm. Arrive early. Walk the space. Memorize the stillness. Carry it with you. “You don’t create calm in the storm. You retrieve it from the morning before.”

    Study · Day 19

    What the research found, and what it did not

    The book says imposter syndrome is “a memory problem”, and that a quiet morning can be stored in the body and spent later. Here is what has been measured, with the papers so you can read them yourself.

    Memory leans toward the bad

    A wide review found the same pattern across everyday events, close relationships, feedback and learning: bad events, bad feedback and bad emotions have more impact than good ones, and bad information is worked over more thoroughly. The authors could find hardly any exceptions.

    What it did not show: it is a review of other people’s studies, not a test of a notebook. It explains why the wins have to be written down: they do not keep themselves.

    Baumeister, R. F., Bratslavsky, E., Finkenauer, C., & Vohs, K. D. (2001). Bad is stronger than good. Review of General Psychology, 5(4), 323–370. doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.5.4.323

    Where confidence comes from

    The founding paper on self-efficacy names four sources of a person’s belief that they can do a thing: what they have actually accomplished, watching others, being told, and the state of the body. The more dependable the source, the stronger the belief, and what you have done yourself is the most dependable. That belief decides how much effort is spent and how long it lasts when things get hard.

    What it did not show: it is a theory paper. Later research tested it; this paper says nothing about a museum as such.

    Bandura, A. (1977). Self-efficacy: Toward a unifying theory of behavioral change. Psychological Review, 84(2), 191–215. doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.84.2.191

    Rehearsing in the mind

    A meta-analysis of the studies on mental practice (going through a task in the mind before doing it) found a positive and significant effect on performance. How much it helped depended on the kind of task, on how long before the performance it was done, and on how long the rehearsal lasted.

    What it did not show: the tool page says the nervous system “cannot tell the difference” between the real and the vividly imagined. This analysis does not go that far. Walking the empty road, as Tonas does, is the real thing and the rehearsal at once.

    Driskell, J. E., Copper, C., & Moran, A. (1994). Does mental practice enhance performance? Journal of Applied Psychology, 79(4), 481–492. doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.79.4.481

    Rest is part of the plan

    Sport scientists from many fields met in 2016 and published what they agree on: a balance between stress and recovery is essential for an athlete to keep performing at a high level, and recovery should be watched and built into the routine, to prevent underrecovery, overtraining, injury and illness.

    What it did not show: it is a statement of expert agreement about athletes, not a trial of the five steps of Seijaku.

    Kellmann, M., Bertollo, M., Bosquet, L., Brink, M., Coutts, A. J., Duffield, R., Erlacher, D., Halson, S. L., Hecksteden, A., Heidari, J., Kallus, K. W., Meeusen, R., Mujika, I., Robazza, C., Skorski, S., Venter, R., & Beckmann, J. (2018). Recovery and performance in sport: Consensus statement. International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance, 13(2), 240–245. doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2017-0759

    Where the Museum of Proof and Seijaku sit in this

    No study here tested a museum of proof by that name. What the notebook holds is what the research calls accomplishments, written down against a memory that leans toward the bad. “Confidence is not a feeling – it is a conclusion based on data” is the book’s line, not a finding.

    Seijaku, the fallow field and Keh Yazh’s rest between runs are teachings of three peoples. The science agrees with their direction; it did not test their five steps.

    This is a practice, not medical care.
