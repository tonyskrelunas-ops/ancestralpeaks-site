    Today's challenge · Tool 1 of 22

    The Stone Breath

    Find a small, smooth stone. Hold it and breathe: in four, hold four, out six, hold four. Five rounds. That is the whole challenge for today.

    The tool, as the book gives it

    Origin: Navajo. Keh Yazh (Sore Foot), champion runner.
    Lineage: Keh Yazh → Masan → Tonas → you.

    Find a small, smooth stone — river stones are best. Keep it in your pocket. When the world gets loud — before a meeting, after a difficult conversation, during a moment of doubt — take the stone in your hand. Hold it lightly. Feel how that simple act softens your jaw and relaxes your face.

    Breathe in, light and easy through your mouth and nose — four counts. Hold — four. Out — six. Hold — four. Repeat five times.

    Open your eyes. Return the stone to your pocket. You are ready.

    The stone is always in your hand. In the old tradition, young runners were taught to imagine the stone on the tongue: the image of it, held still, kept the jaw soft and the face calm under pressure. The tongue is imagination, not instruction. You never actually put anything in your mouth.

    From “The Complete Toolkit” in Stone Breath.

    Study · Day 1

    What the research found, and what it did not

    The runners had the practice long before there was a laboratory to look at it. Here is what has been measured since, in plain words, with the papers so you can read them yourself.

    Slow breathing changes the body's settings

    A systematic review looked at breathing slowed below ten breaths a minute in healthy people. Out of 2,461 abstracts, 15 studies met its bar. Across them, slow breathing went with higher heart rate variability and with people reporting more comfort, relaxation and alertness, and less arousal, anxiety and anger.

    What it did not show: how it works. The authors say the mechanism is still under debate, and fifteen studies is a small base.

    Zaccaro, A., Piarulli, A., Laurino, M., Garbella, E., Menicucci, D., Neri, B., & Gemignani, A. (2018). How breath-control can change your life: A systematic review on psycho-physiological correlates of slow breathing. Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 12, 353. doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00353

    The heart's rhythm answers the breath

    A larger review and meta-analysis gathered 223 studies of voluntary slow breathing. Heart rate variability linked to the vagus nerve rose during the breathing, right after a single session, and after weeks of practice. The authors call it low-tech and low-cost, with few adverse effects expected.

    What it did not show: heart rate variability is a sign the calming side of the nervous system is at work. It is not, by itself, proof of better health.

    Laborde, S., Allen, M. S., Borges, U., Dosseville, F., Hosang, T. J., Iskra, M., Mosley, E., Salvotti, C., Spolverato, L., Zammit, N., & Javelle, F. (2022). Effects of voluntary slow breathing on heart rate and heart rate variability: A systematic review and a meta-analysis. Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews, 138, 104711. doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2022.104711

    Five minutes a day, with the long breath out

    A randomized, controlled study gave people one of three five-minute breathing exercises, or mindfulness meditation, every day for a month. Breathing practice, and most of all the kind built on a long breath out, improved mood more and slowed the resting breath more than the meditation did.

    What it did not show: the exercise that did best was cyclic sighing, not the Stone Breath. The study ran one month, from home, and mood was reported by the people themselves.

    Balban, M. Y., Neri, E., Kogon, M. M., Weed, L., Nouriani, B., Jo, B., Holl, G., Zeitzer, J. M., Spiegel, D., & Huberman, A. D. (2023). Brief structured respiration practices enhance mood and reduce physiological arousal. Cell Reports Medicine, 4(1), 100895. doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895

    A real effect, and a warning about hype

    A meta-analysis of randomized trials (12 trials, 785 adults for stress) found that breathing practice lowered self-reported stress by a small-to-medium amount compared with control groups, with similar results for anxiety and low mood.

    What it did not show: most of the trials carried a moderate risk of bias, and the authors ask for better studies before large claims are made. That caution belongs on this page too.

    Fincham, G. W., Strauss, C., Montero-Marin, J., & Cavanagh, K. (2023). Effect of breathwork on stress and mental health: A meta-analysis of randomised-controlled trials. Scientific Reports, 13, 432. doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-27247-y

    Where the Stone Breath sits in this

    One round of the Stone Breath is eighteen counts. At about a second a count, that is a little over three breaths a minute, well inside what these studies call slow breathing, and its breath out is longer than its breath in.

    No study here tested the Stone Breath itself, with its two holds and its stone. The stone in the hand, the soft jaw and the image on the tongue come from the runners, not from a laboratory. One theory of why the long breath out calms the body is that it works through the vagus nerve; its authors present it as a model to be tested, not a settled fact.

    Gerritsen, R. J. S., & Band, G. P. H. (2018). Breath of life: The respiratory vagal stimulation model of contemplative activity. Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 12, 397. doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00397

    This is a practice, not medical care. If holding the breath makes you dizzy or uneasy, leave the holds out and keep the long breath out.

    The passage to keep

    “Hold this. Breathe into it — in light and easy, out long and slow. You are not dying. Your body just thinks you are, and there is a difference.”

    “Before we look at anything you have been carrying, your body has to believe it is safe. That is not the warm-up. That is the work. You cannot reprogram a mind that is running for its life.”

    Tonas, on the way down. Stone Breath, Chapter 1.

    Three questions to think on or write about

    1. Where in an ordinary day does your body think it is dying when it is not? What does it do first: the jaw, the shoulders, the breath?
    2. Tonas says the marks on the canyon wall are not damage but formation. Which mark of your own could you ask a different question: “What were you carving?”
    3. “You were only rich if other people were carrying the same knowledge.” What do you carry that no one else carries yet, and who could you hand it to?
