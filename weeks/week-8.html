Week 8 of 8 · with Kiprono

Say the Names Out Loud

Step seven: attach it to something that recurs.

Member preview. In the live path, each week opens in order, with its film, its guided audio and its circle.

Before you begin

What this week is for. The last step of the method: "Attach it to something that recurs. Knowledge that depends on willingness decays at the speed of one distracted generation. Knowledge bolted to a calendar, a ceremony, a season or an obligation does not." This week you find one thing that lives in only one head, and you give it a place in a year that already comes around.

Your guide this week is Kiprono. A herder and an elder of the Rift Valley. Forty-one cattle, and a name for each one. To him a name is never only a name. It is the handle on everything he knows about that animal and her line. He thinks in generations, and he teaches the long line: you do not carry it the whole way, only far enough to hand it on.

What you need: one person in mind. The calendar you already keep. A phone that records, and paper, because two is better than one. One hour that already exists.

The one rule, with no exceptions: no resolutions. Whatever you take on this week rides on something that already comes around.

The reading (Day 1)

Read the chapter in one sitting: One Hundred and Eight. It is the one chapter about a carry that did not hold, and it says so plainly: "The woman in this story is invented. The numbers are not, and they are the most important numbers in this book."

The story is a mother and her jars. Rice on the shelf above the fire, a different rice in every jar, and a woman who "could tell them apart in the dark." Her daughter goes down to the coast for school, comes home one dry season, and asks, kindly, why her mother does not just plant the one that gives the most. The mother has no answer ready. Then the counting: a man with a notebook, one hundred and eight names, forty-six found. Then the call, the one planting, the recorder, and sixty-one names saved.

One note on respect. The chapter names the Blaan rituals and depicts none of them: "I have not depicted one and I will not." This week holds the same line. Nothing here describes a ceremony, and nothing should.

The line to carry this week: "It is hard to lose a thing the year itself keeps asking for."

The science, in five minutes

Every number below is real, and sourced in the chapter's notes.

  • The count. "A survey of 42 Blaan farmers in Sarangani Province, Mindanao, produced 108 named rice varieties." A collection effort then found and documented 46. Sixty-two were reported as diminishing. Put plainly: "in one documented case, a people could still name more than twice as many varieties as still existed."
  • The order of loss. "The seed was not lost first. The practice was." The chain runs: "Ceremony thins → the names stop being said → the varieties stop being planted → the seed stops being viable → the variety is gone." And: "Each step is small. None of them is a decision anybody made."
  • Ceremony was the storage. Rites bound to the farming year were "the maintenance schedule for the knowledge." "A ceremony fixed to a season is an obligation that recurs whether or not anyone feels like it." When it stops, nothing visible happens for years. "What has stopped is the rehearsal, and rehearsal was the only thing holding a hundred and eight names in a population's memory."
  • The same architecture, once more. The Tboli, neighbors of the Blaan, time planting and harvest by the sky and the land: "The sky as calendar. The land as index." It is the pattern this path has met in the Pacific, the Sahara, the Andes and the high desert.
  • The controlled comparison. In week one you read the line that never broke. This is the line that broke. "The variable is not the quality of the knowledge." "The variable is whether the thing that forced the knowledge to be performed kept forcing it."
  • Run it on yourself. The chapter turns the diagnostic toward the reader: "The Sunday that stopped. The craft nobody apprentices into." The documents are still on file and the old person is still findable, so the loss stays invisible until somebody asks what that one is for.

The practice: seven days

Day 1 · Find your hundred and eight. After the reading: "Think of one person who holds something nobody has written down." A parent or grandparent. A colleague near the end of a career. A neighbor who is the last one who knows how the thing actually works. Or yourself, about something you have never taught anyone. Write the name on a card, and under it, honestly, how much of what they hold exists outside their head. "For most of what matters, the answer is none."

Day 2 · The jars are not the knowledge. Walk through your home or your workplace and find the jars: the photographs, the recipe card, the files, the tools in the workshop. Pick one you could not read on your own. "The knowledge is the name, said out loud, by somebody who knows what it is for." And: "A jar you cannot read is grain. A recipe nobody has cooked is paper."

Day 3 · Make the year ask. "This is the whole chapter and it is the one step people skip." No resolution. Open your calendar and find one thing that already comes around: the birthday, the Sunday call, the anniversary, the first weekend of the season, the standing meeting. Give it a new job. "Not an hour you have to find — an hour that already exists, with a new job given to it." Write the recurring thing on your card beside the name.

Day 4 · Set the first one, this month. "Not someday. This month." Ask the person for an hour inside the recurring thing you chose. Write down the questions that get the stack and not just the name: "not what is this, but what is it for, and when do you use it, and what happens if you get it wrong?" Bring a recorder and paper both, "because two is better than one." What they give you stays theirs. If anything they hold belongs to the sacred life of their people, they decide what is recorded, and you follow their lead.

Day 5 · Try the stack on yourself. Before you sit down with them, be your own first source. Pick one thing you know and have never taught anyone. Say its name into the recorder, then answer the three questions out loud. Notice where you can almost get it and cannot quite. You will not get all of it, from yourself or from them. "That is not failure. That is the sixty-one."

Day 6 · Say one out loud in front of somebody younger. One name, with what it is for: a child, a grandchild, the newest person on the team. Do not make it a lesson. Say it where they can hear it. "That is all transmission has ever been." "Not an archive. Not a plan."

Day 7 · All of it on one card. The person. The jar you cannot read. The recurring thing that will carry it. The date of the first sitting, this month. The first name you said out loud, and who heard it. Put the card where you will see it the next time the recurring thing comes around. Then the two lines the tool ends on: "We are too late for most of it and we are not too late for all of it." And: "Go and get the sixty-one."

This week's reading (text and audio)

The Ones Who Carried You. About five minutes aloud.

The stone in your hand came a long way to reach you. So did you.

Members get the full text and a recording to listen to on a walk or a drive this week.

Kiprono's film for the week (about 90 seconds)

[Then. A smoke-darkened shelf above a cooking fire, lined end to end with jars. A woman's hand goes into one and rolls a few grains between her fingers.] Each one had rice in it and each one was a different rice. She could tell them apart in the dark. [An upland rice field on a slope at first light. Wind through the stalks. No people in frame.] The name was the handle. Everything else was underneath. [The shelf again, years later. Every jar still there, dust on the lids.] The jars stayed on the shelf. A hundred and eight in our mouths. Forty-six in the world. [Now. Evening under a wide tree in the Rift Valley. Kiprono walks slowly among his cattle, his granddaughter beside him. He lays a hand on each animal and says its name. She says it back.] It is hard to lose a thing the year itself keeps asking for. [He stops at the next one and waits. She says the name before he does.] A person saying a name out loud, in front of someone who is not paying attention and is learning it anyway. [Ahead. Morning at the same fence. The girl crouches beside a smaller child and points to a calf. She says its name. The little one says it back.] That is all transmission has ever been. [Close on the small child, saying the name again to the calf.] She knew the name, and she said it out loud.

Every narrated line is from the chapter.

The circle (end of the week)

One question, and anyone may pass:

Say one name out loud: something you know only because somebody kept saying it in front of you.

If there is time, a second round: What recurring thing did you choose, and who is your one person?

Circle rules: nobody is asked how they felt; invite, never conscript; silence is not a problem to be solved.

For your work

  • The colleague two years from retiring: find your hundred and eight. Ask what it is for, when it is used, and what happens if it goes wrong. The manual on the shared drive is a jar.
  • The meeting already on the calendar: make the year ask. Give ten minutes of the standing meeting to one name, said by the person who holds it. No new meeting, no new project.
  • The handover nobody sits in anymore: "The handover that used to happen because two people sat in the same room and now does not because nobody sits in the same room." Put two people back in one room, once a season.
  • The newest person on the team: say one out loud in front of them. Not a training deck. A name and what it is for, in a room where they happen to be.

The end of the path, and the beginning

Eight weeks ago you began with the first step: settle the carrier first. You end with the seventh: attach it to something that recurs. The calm came first. Now it has a place in the year.

The book's last pages name the messenger all of this was shaping: "more powerful and more calm than any messenger who has ever lived." It sounds like a contradiction. It is not. "Because these two things were never opposites — they are the same thing." And: "The calm is not what holds the power back. The calm is what lets the power out."

That is what these eight weeks were for. Not a shelf of tools to admire, but one person, capable and steady at once: "Fierce and calm at once. Powerful because peaceful." The old crossers knew it: "the quiet ones went farthest not in spite of their quiet, but because of it."

Keep coming to the circle. The path ends here. The practice does not. Bring your card, your one person, and whatever you got of their sixty-one.

Teach one person. This is Kiprono's way: not everything, not everyone. One tool from these eight weeks, to one person, inside something that already comes around. You will not hand on all of it. Hand on what you have, and let the year keep asking.

The book closes on four lines. They are yours now:

Watch the world for longer than your own life. Carry what you see to the people who need it. Write it where it will outlast you. And run.

Member note: this is practice, not medical care. If something in this week stirs more than you can carry, bring it to the circle or to someone who can help.
