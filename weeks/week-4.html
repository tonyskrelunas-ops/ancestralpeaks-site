Week 4 of 8 · with Chariya

Build One Songline

Step three: encode for a degraded operator.

Member preview. In the live path, each week opens in order, with its film, its guided audio and its circle.

Before you begin

What this week is for. Step three of the method every tradition in this path arrived at: "Encode for a degraded operator." The old carriers stored what mattered as named places in order: "Everything is stored as discrete named states in sequence." This week you build one of your own, on ground your feet already know.

Your guide this week is Chariya. She farms the rice terraces her people cut into the hills. On a terrace, the ground holds the plan: the water reaches the top field first and then steps down, field by field, in an order nobody has to look up. She carries her land in her feet, not on a map. She is the plainest speaker of the eight guides, and she will not let anyone call a working method magic.

What you need: a route your feet know, with a fixed order. The walk from your door to the shop is ideal. A card and a pen. Thirty minutes one evening to build it, and a few minutes on the other days.

The one rule, with no exceptions: build on your own ground. This week borrows a structure, never a song. No one else's places, verses or meanings, and nobody is owed them.

The reading (Day 1)

Read the chapter in one sitting: The Ones Who Decided to Show Us. It is built unlike any other chapter, and says so in its first line: "This chapter breaks the book's form, on purpose." There is no carrier's head to sit inside, no narrator, no verse and no named site. Only the structure the custodians themselves put into the public record, and what it cost them to do that.

The first part is the structure. Picture a route that crosses the countries of many peoples. "Now imagine the route is a song." Then the turn: "Not a song about the route. The route, as a song." Each verse is tied to a place, and carries what is there. "The country is the index. The song is the retrieval." And one thing the other systems do not have: each community is custodian of its own stretch. "So the full line exists, and no single person holds it."

The second part is what it cost to show us. "The Seven Sisters songline is one of the very few that has been extensively documented — and it was done in collaboration with Traditional Owners." Then: "That did not happen to those people. They decided it." Senior custodians weighed it and judged that "the risk of it being lost is now greater than the risk of it being shared." The chapter sets it beside the Pacific master who gave his rank away in time: "This is the same calculation, made by a different people, on a different continent, in a different century, about a different body of knowledge." And they kept the terms: "What was shared was shared by its owners. What was not shared was not shared, and nobody is owed it."

Then read the limits box. "That restraint is not an apology for a thin chapter. It is the chapter."

The line to carry this week: "You put the list into a place you already carry, and the place did the work."

The science, in five minutes

Every claim below is sourced in the notes at the back.

  • Place is what memory keeps best. "The hippocampal system encodes place with unusual durability, and sequences of locations are among the most robust structures human memory supports." The memory palace of Western antiquity is "a thin, late, individual version of the same principle."
  • Named places beat a running total. "A running total held by a tired, cold, frightened person drifts and gives no warning." Ten named places in order can be checked one at a time.
  • Absurd sticks. "The sillier it is, the more it will stick, and that is not a trick, it is how the encoding works."
  • The ground refreshes it. "A memory palace is reconstructed from the inside. A songline is re-run through the actual ground, which means the index is refreshed every time anyone travels it, and errors are caught against the country itself."
  • The ground checks it. "A wrong verse is wrong against the world, and the world does not negotiate." Your bench is still there in the morning, whatever your memory says. "The desert is not persuadable."
  • Use is the storage. "A songline that is not sung is not preserved at reduced quality." At the scale of your corner shop, the same holds: walk it, and it stays.

The practice: seven days

Day 1 · Pick the route. After the reading, choose a route your body knows, not one you know from a map: the walk from your door to the shop, the house you grew up in room by room, the drive you have done a thousand times. One test: "It must have an order and the order must be fixed."

Day 2 · Ten places. Break the route into about ten places: the gate, the corner with the broken curb, the big tree, the crossing, the bench. Name them. Say them out loud, in order, twice. Write them down the left side of your card.

Day 3 · Ten things. Choose the ten things you need to carry: a talk you have to give, a list you keep losing, the names of the people you will meet, the steps of a process you keep looking up. Pick something real and coming up soon. Write the ten down the right side of the card, in order.

Day 4 · Make it absurd, then walk it. This is the thirty-minute build. Put one thing at each place, and not neatly. "The thing at the gate should be doing something at the gate. Big, loud, moving, ridiculous." Then walk the route in your mind, start to finish. Twice tonight. Put the card away.

Day 5 · The morning test. Before you get out of bed, walk the route and say the ten things. "You will have it. Almost everyone does, first time, and the common reaction is a kind of irritation — why was I never told this?" If a place comes up empty, do not start over. Go back to the last place you were sure of and walk forward from there. Make the missing picture bigger and louder, and walk it once more.

Day 6 · Walk it with your feet. The real route, saying the ten things at the ten places, under your breath if anyone is near. "It will feel strange and it will be noticeably stronger than doing it in your head." Notice where the real ground corrects you. That is the audit.

Day 7 · Use it, then honor it. Put the songline to work on the thing you built it for: give the talk without notes, greet the people by name, run the process without looking it up. Walk the route once more, so it stays. Then read the chapter's last lines again: "You have just used, badly and for twenty minutes, a technology that other people have run for tens of thousands of years, across a continent, without losing it." Then the instruction it ends on: "Treat it with the respect that deserves."

This week's reading (text and audio)

The Water Remembers. About seven minutes aloud.

A grandfather called to sing an old song at a mountain lake: water has a memory, and so do we.

Members get the full text and a recording to listen to on a walk or a drive this week.

Chariya's film for the week (about 90 seconds)

[Then. First light over wide, open country: grass, stone, a line of low hills. No people, no landmarks. The camera moves slowly from one feature to the next, in order.] A person cannot carry a continent in their head as a map. Nobody can. [The light reaches the next ridge, and the next.] The landscape does the work that a filing system does, and the landscape cannot be lost, corrupted, burned or forgotten, because it is made of ground. [Now. Before breakfast, Chariya steps out of her front door and walks to the shop. The gate. The corner with the broken curb. The big tree. The crossing. The bench.] Pick a route you know in your body. It must have an order and the order must be fixed. [At the gate she stops and laughs at something only she can see.] Big, loud, moving, ridiculous. [The same walk anywhere: a city door, a corner, a corner shop. At the counter, a member names a list from memory, one finger counting off places.] You put the list into a place you already carry, and the place did the work. [Chariya walks home the same way, saying the ten things at the ten places under her breath.] The country is not a mnemonic aid to the knowledge. The country is where the knowledge lives. [Ahead. A child walks the route beside her and names each place before she does.] Thirty minutes to build. Then it is yours for life. [Back to the open country at full light, empty and still.] Treat it with the respect that deserves.

Every narrated line is from the chapter. Nothing in the film shows Aboriginal people, ceremonies or sacred sites: the land at dawn is open country with no landmarks, and the route is an ordinary walk from a door to a shop.

The circle (end of the week)

One question, and anyone may pass:

Name the ten places on your route, in order, and the most ridiculous thing you put at one of them.

If there is time, a second round: Where did a place come up empty, and what brought it back?

Circle rules: nobody is asked how they felt; invite, never conscript; silence is not a problem to be solved.

For your work

  • Before the talk: ten points on ten places. Walk the route in your head and give it without notes. If you lose the thread, go back to the last place you were sure of.
  • Before the room full of new people: put each person's name at a place, and make the picture absurd. Walk the route on your way in.
  • The process you keep looking up: put its steps on your route, in order. When something goes wrong, you can find the exact step where it broke: "A sequence of named checkpoints can be audited from the last certain one."
  • On the site visit: walk the real space with your team and say each step where it will happen. A plan that is wrong is wrong against the floor, and the floor does not negotiate.
